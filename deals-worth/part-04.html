Title: "Deal's Worth"

Author: Jeffrey Robert Palin Jr.

Chapter 1 : Eric's Deal

The last ordinary thing Eric did was laugh at a lamp.

It was in the window of the fourth trinket shop of the afternoon, a brass thing shaped like a duck wearing what appeared to be a very small business suit, and it cost more than his car payment. Amy pressed her forehead to the glass and said it had "presence". Eric said it had "a lawyer". She laughed hard enough that a man walking a terrier looked over, and Eric filed the sound away the way he always did, without knowing he was filing anything, because that's the thing nobody tells you about the last ordinary thing. It doesn't announce itself. It just stands there in a window, wearing a tiny suit.

The day was cloudless. He remembered that afterward with a kind of resentment. A cloudless day feels like a promise.

At the crossing, Amy stepped off the curb first, the way she always did, three steps ahead and impatient with the world for being slower than her. Then she stopped, turned, and looked back at him — that look, chin down, eyebrows up, half a dare — and put her hand out.

Eric raised his eyebrows back at her. He took her hand.

The horn arrived before the car did.

Not a warning. A verdict. It came in flat and enormous and "already close", and Eric's body understood the geometry of it a full second before his mind caught up: the angle, the speed, the absolute arithmetic of where that grille was going to be and where Amy was currently standing, which were the same place.

He had time for exactly one decision.

He made it with his hands.

---

Sound came back before anything else, and it came back wrong — underwater, edge-blurred, like the world was being played through a wall. Somewhere in it, Amy was screaming for someone to call an ambulance, and it wasn't her voice. Eric had known her nine years and had never heard that sound come out of her.

Strangers were already dialing. Somebody was telling her not to move him. Somebody else was telling her he was still breathing, look, he's still breathing, and Eric wanted to tell them that he agreed, that he was in fact still breathing, that this was a real cornerstone of his current strategy —

and then the light tipped sideways and went out.

---

He woke up into the shape of a room.

That was the only way to describe it. He couldn't see the room; his eyes wouldn't open, and after a while he understood in a distant, administrative way that they weren't going to. But he could hear it — the pressurized hiss, the rhythmic chirp of something keeping score — and he could "feel" it with a clarity that would have been beautiful under literally any other circumstances.

He could feel the sheet. He could feel the tape on the back of his hand and the cold creep of whatever was going into it. He could feel the specific weight of Amy's forehead when she put it down on his forearm and left it there, and the wet spot that spread under it, and the exact moment she lifted her head and wiped her face and made herself sound normal before she spoke.

Everything worked. "Everything worked". The touch, the temperature, the pain — all of it, head to feet, reporting in perfectly.

The only thing that didn't work was the part where any of it got back out.

---

A doctor came in on the fourth day and asked Amy's permission to see whether Eric was in there.

They explained it gently, the way you explain things to someone who has slept in a vinyl chair for ninety hours. There were indicators. Rapid eye movement under the lids. Patterns that didn't match a body that had nobody home. They wanted to run stimulus tests. They wanted to count.

"Eric," the doctor said, close to his ear, slow and loud in the way that people get slow and loud at the unconscious, "I'm going to count up from zero. When I reach three, I want you to move a finger. Any finger. Just once."

"Oh", Eric thought, with the first genuine emotion he'd had in four days. "Oh, this is going to be great".

"One."

"Not that one".

"Two."

"Definitely not that one".

"Three."

Eric moved his middle finger.

There was a pause in the room that he would treasure for the rest of his life.

"...Again," the doctor said. "Zero. One. Two. Three. Four. Five — "

Eric, with enormous care and the only muscle group he had left in the world, moved his middle finger five times.

"That's — " The doctor cleared his throat. "That's a very consistent response."

"That's not a response," Amy said. Her voice had gone strange. "That's him. That's 'him', that's him doing that on purpose, he's — " and then she was laughing, which turned into something else halfway through, and a nurse put a hand on her shoulder and she leaned into it and cried so hard she had to sit down on the floor.

It was the best conversation they'd had in four days. It was, technically, the only one.

Over the next week they mapped him the rest of the way. Touch, everywhere. Pain, everywhere. Full sensory function in a body that would not answer the phone.

The word the specialists settled on was "minimally conscious", which Eric found rude. He was maximally conscious. He was arguably the most conscious he had ever been in his life, lying there with nothing to do but be awake in a body that had become a very expensive coffin with excellent insulation.

Three weeks in, nothing had changed except the flowers.

---

Eric heard the briefcase before he heard the man.

It was a specific sound, a hard-sided case being set down on a hospital floor with the confidence of someone who has done it in a great many hospitals. Amy had been mid-sentence, reading him a news article about a lottery winner, which she'd started doing because the silence had begun to feel like an accusation.

"Hi," Amy said, in the flattened voice of a woman who has spent three weeks being approached by people with clipboards.

"Hello." The man's voice was pleasant and unhurried. "I'm aware of the situation you and your husband are in. I'm here to propose a solution."

A pause.

"Forgive me," Amy said. "Who, may I ask, are you?"

"Oh my." A rustle, almost sheepish. "Forgive my rudeness. I'm with the VRC."

"...VRC?"

"The Virtual Reality Corporation. We're not a government body but a private organization." A chair creaked as he sat, unbidden. "We've been quietly running hospital partnerships nationwide. In fact, you're standing in one of the hospitals. We've developed a new class of technology aimed specifically at patients in particular physical medical conditions, and we're in a testing phase, which means implementation would be at no cost to you. What it would offer, Mrs. — " a small pause, which Eric noticed, because the man already knew, " — what it would offer is 'communication'. Interaction. With your husband. Substantially more of it than a counted finger."

From the bed, Eric thought, very clearly and with great force: "keep talking".

"It would require multiple surgeries," the man went on, because of course it would. "Including brain surgery."

Amy didn't answer for a long time. Eric knew that silence intimately. It was the silence of Amy looking at him, at his hands lying on the blanket like two objects she was supposed to recognize.

"If my husband can respond," she said finally, "does he get a say in this?"

"Of course he does."

"Wrong answer", Eric thought — not because it was wrong, but because it had come too fast. It had come out the way a line comes out when it's been said before, in this room, in other rooms, to other people in vinyl chairs.

He filed that away too.

---

They talked it over for days, and to their credit they did it in front of him. Amy insisted. She'd pull her chair to the bedside and say "okay, tell him the part about the interface again", and the man in the suit would tell him the part about the interface again, patiently, never once slipping into the third person the way everyone else did. Eric came to appreciate that about him. It was almost enough to distract from the fact that the man had answers ready for questions Amy hadn't asked yet.

On the sixth day, Amy said yes.

The paperwork went on for a long time. She read all of it, out loud, so he could hear it, asking questions in the gaps so he could listen to the answers. It aligned with everything they'd discussed. It also expanded on it in the way paperwork always expands on it, in appendices, in language that had clearly been through a room full of lawyers and come out the other side smooth as a river stone.

She signed her name twice. Once for herself. Once on his behalf, because he was documented as unable to consent, which was true in every legal sense and false in every sense that mattered to him.

He heard the pen. It was a good pen. Heavy. The kind you bring when you expect people to sign things.

They scheduled the surgery for the next morning, which gave Amy the night to call everyone. His mother came. His brother came. Somebody brought a folding chair from a waiting room two floors down and nobody slept, and around 3 a.m. his brother started telling a story about a canoe that Eric had heard eleven hundred times and had never once been so glad to hear.

"He'll be ready by noon tomorrow," the man in the suit said.

He was ready by noon.

---

Afterward, Eric's head was a landscape he didn't recognize. Not painful, exactly. Wrong-shaped. Like a room where someone had moved all the furniture two inches to the left while he was out.

He couldn't see what he'd become, but he could infer it from sound. He had acquired a "fleet". Machines on all sides, each with its own opinion, and somewhere under them, threaded into him at the neck and skull and spine, a whole new set of connections that didn't do anything yet.

"We'll connect the tablet to Eric's network now," the man said.

"Okay." Amy's voice was very small. "Okay."

"You'll be able to see and hear the session. That's all, for this phase. You won't be able to participate."

"Okay," Amy said again, and then, barely above breath, she started praying. Eric hadn't known she still did that. He lay there in the dark of himself, feeling the tape pull on his hand, listening to his wife negotiate with a God she wasn't sure about on behalf of a husband she wasn't sure about either, and he thought: "whatever this is, be worth it. For her. Be worth it".

Something in the room changed pitch.

"Beginning," the man said.

---

Eric was alone in his own head, thinking about the duck lamp, when someone else said something in it.

"Testing. Can you hear me?"

He did not so much hear it as "have" it. It arrived the way his own thoughts arrived — from nowhere, fully formed, in the voice he used for thinking — except it wasn't his. It had a different weight. A different accent, if thoughts could have one.

"...So to speak, of course. Nothing mental is actual audio. The mind never produces sound waves. But you'll find 'hear' is the closest word available, and I'd rather not spend our first minute on vocabulary".

Eric's reaction was instantaneous and entirely internal and consisted mostly of profanity.

"That was my mental voice. That was my own voice and I did not say it. I'm not picturing this. I'm not doing this — I am hallucinating. Three weeks in a bed and my brain has finally filed for divorce —"

"You're not hallucinating. Though I understand why that's the first place you went. Most people go there". A pause, tolerant. "We can transmit physical, artificial "custom signals" directly into your brain through the interface setup you received this morning. Those signals force your brain to imagine and manifest my communications. That's the whole trick. It isn't elegant, but it works, and it works because a considerable portion of your nervous system is now wired into our systems".

"You're making my brain imagine things", Eric thought slowly, "and using that as a phone line".

"Yes".

"That's — ok. Ok. That's actually incredible". He turned it over. "Why does it need the surgery? Why not just — beam it in?"

"The VRC has never achieved this wirelessly. Not once. Everything we do of this kind, we do through surgically implanted interfaces". Something that might have been dry humor. "Believe me, we've tried. It would save us an enormous amount on anesthesiologists".

Eric had been awake and alone for three weeks. He had composed, in that time, approximately four hundred hours of unsendable material. And now there was a door in the wall of his own skull.

"Okay", he thought. "Okay. Who are you, and what do you want?"

"I want to determine whether you and your wife are worthy".

"...Worthy of what?"

"Worthy of what you're being offered".

"That's not an answer. That's a fortune cookie with a legal department".

"It's the answer you get before the interview. You'll have the rest after".

And then the interview started, and it did not stop for a very long time.

---

It was not a conversation. It was an excavation.

The voice asked him about his mother. It asked him about a job he'd quit in his twenties and the specific reason he'd given versus the specific reason he'd had. It asked what he thought happened to people when they died, then asked again forty questions later in different words to see whether the answer had moved. It asked what he would do if he could do anything, and then — this was the part that made Eric's skin crawl in a body that couldn't crawl — it asked "follow-ups that were too good".

Three things about it stopped feeling like a person.

The first was speed. It answered instantly. Not quickly. "Instantly", in the gap where a human being would still be inhaling.

The second was worse. It kept answering the thought he was "still assembling" — the half-built one, the one he hadn't found the words for yet, the raw shape of the thing before it became a sentence. Twice it responded to a question Eric abandoned before finishing. Once it responded to an objection he decided not to raise.

The third was the repetition. Ask a question two different ways and get the same reply both times, phrased identically, with the same little pause in the same little place. Like a drawer being opened. Like a script with excellent coverage.

Somewhere in hour two, Eric stopped thinking of it as "the man" and started thinking of it as something else, and once the word arrived he couldn't get rid of it.

"The entity".

He was careful not to think it too loudly. Then he remembered that was not a thing he could control, and thought it as loudly as possible out of spite.

"You're not the first", the entity said, unbothered. "Many people have sat where you're sitting. So to speak. And 'the entity' isn't the worst one I've been called this month".

---

What it told him next, it told him in the same order it had told Amy, which Eric appreciated more than he expected. It meant she hadn't been sold a different version.

His brain worked. His body didn't. That gap, the entity explained, was the entire business model.

Until his condition resolved — if it resolved — Eric would participate in an advanced computer simulation, running on VRC systems, populated by others. He would interact with them. He would live there. Staff would monitor the environment through displays, speakers, monitors, whatever was convenient.

"And before you ask: there will be no glitches. No anomalies. No corrupted geometry, no character falling through the floor, no moment where the seams show. Our technology is mastered to the point that such things are not possible in anything connected to VRC computers".

"Nothing's that stable", Eric thought.

"Ours is".

It wasn't said like a boast. That was the part Eric didn't like. It was said the way you'd say "water is wet" — as a condition of the universe, established long before you got here, not up for discussion.

The realism, the entity went on, came from the same trick as the conversation. Specialized computers, wired into his brain and body, transmitting impulses directly to his somatosensory cortex — indistinguishable from the signals real touch produces, because they "were" the signals real touch produces. He would feel contact. Texture. Weight. Heat.

"We also make extensive use of what you'd know as phantom limb syndrome. The brain can be convinced to feel a limb that isn't there. We simply do it on purpose and at scale. Internally we call it 'phantom undergoings'."

"You can make me feel a hand I don't have".

"We can make you feel a hand you've never had. Users routinely operate additional arms. Centaur legs. Wings. Tails. The technology has been refined to the point that people control these as naturally as the limbs they were born with. It takes most of them about a day. The tails take longer. Tails are surprisingly hard".

Eric, who currently could not operate a single one of his original four limbs, found that he had to stop and breathe. Or do whatever the equivalent was, in there.

"Say the rest", he thought. "There's a catch. There's always a catch and you've been circling it for an hour".

"There's no catch. There are specifications. You'll find the difference matters".

---

The specification was this: the simulation could not perfectly reproduce the real universe.

"The physics are close. The quantum mechanics are not exact and never will be. We don't pretend otherwise". A pause. "We find most people stop minding once magic is on the table."

"Magic".

"Optional. Per universe. We'll come back to it".

What makes the simulation "extremely" interesting, the entity said, is the genuine unpredictability — real randomness, not a random number generator — because the whole system was continuously perturbed from outside by non-digital hands: maintenance performed by actual people at actual terminals, and every interaction between the simulation and an avatar driven by a living human in the physical world.

"Randomness has to come from somewhere. Ours comes from you. All of you".

And then, mildly, as though it were the next line on a form:

"Using the physical universe as your template, you'll need to specify which laws, modifications, and rules will govern the universe you'll be living in. Physics. Magic. Death. All of it. In detail, in your application".

Eric stopped.

"Say that again".

"Your application".

"The universe I'll be living in".

"Yes".

"Which I'm — writing".
