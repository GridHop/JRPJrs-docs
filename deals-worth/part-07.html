Deal's Worth — Part 7 of 9

The screen showed a canyon from high above, the way a hawk would see one. Sandstone pillars stood up out of the ground like the knuckles of something buried. Glass-like crystals glittered among them in scattered clusters, orange here, green there, one far off throwing back light like a signal mirror. And in the middle of all of it, very small, a figure in red armor was fighting a dragon.

The dragon was not small.

It opened its jaws, and a column of blue flame poured out and missed the armored figure by a body length.

Alicia gestured at the TV with her ring hand. "It's streaming on the main Smart TV app for Sports. Some guy wished for himself to be streamed this morning and it's been streaming since. The view is from high above and follows him."

The sports bars are furious."

She turned the phone toward him without taking her eyes off the screen. A news feed scrolled past. Headlines. Clips of the same footage recorded off people's televisions.

Jeff stared at the tiny armored man, who was shouting at the sky between swings.

A dry voice from the doorway said, "Somebody want to tell the blind man what's happening on the television?"

The room went quiet in a new and uncomfortable way.

"Oh my gosh." Alicia finally looked away from the screen, saw the short bald man in the cardigan and sunglasses, and was off the couch in one motion. "Hi. I'm so sorry. I thought you were a neighbor. I'm Alicia."

"Yogi. I get that a lot."

The dragon's back is doing the thing again," Biscuit said, trotting to the middle of the rug.

"What thing?" Yogi asked.

"Its back plates," Alicia said, and there it was again, the tone. She'd been sitting here for an hour and a half, and she had notes. "Before it does anything that isn't biting or clawing, the plates on its back stand up. Different arrangement every time.

"The armored man turned into lightning," Biscuit said.

"How long's this been going?" Jeff asked.

"Since about noon." Alicia was practically bouncing on her toes. "He was just walking around this canyon on camera, showing off. Posing on rocks. Nobody knew there was a dragon there. He didn't know there was a dragon there. It wasn't in a cave, Jeff. It was one of the rock formations. He walked up and knocked on it with his axe, like knocking on a door, to see how it sounded."

"And it sounded like a dragon."

"It sounded like a dragon."

Jeff watched the dragon track the lightning streak and pivot, it fought like something that had done so many times and had kept track of what worked.

Jeff looked at the screen. At the canyon floor, the pillars, the scattered crystals, the tiny armored man and the huge dragon. He looked at every part of the picture he could.

There was no genie anywhere in it.

"Well," Jeff said, and felt his pulse pick up, and felt the familiar heat bloom behind his eyes. "It just so happens I've got two wishes left."

Alicia spun to face him so fast her hair clip finally gave up and hit the floor.

"You found another genie?"

Jeff pointed both hands at Yogi, like a game show host revealing the car.

"Oh," Alicia said. "Oh, you're— I thought you were a neighbor!"

"You mentioned," Yogi said.

"Your eyes are doing the thing," Biscuit told Jeff.

"They're excited."

"They're very excited."

And then Biscuit went completely still, in the particular way that means everything inside has started moving at once.

"Two," he said.

"Two," Jeff agreed.

"Jeff." Biscuit stood up. "You promised."

"I didn't forget," Jeff said.

He pulled the cyan die out of his pocket and held it up out of habit, for nobody in particular. "Real quick. Yogi, Alicia. Alicia, Yogi. Yogi, Biscuit. Biscuit, Yogi. Found him through this in the woods, he's great, he's blind, he knows where your hand is anyway, don't ask how, he'll say later. Okay."

"Starts with 'I wish,'" Yogi said.

"I know it starts with 'I wish.'" Jeff squared his shoulders. "Yogi. I wish for Biscuit to change into a human."

"Granted," Yogi said.

Light came up out of the rug.

It didn't flash. It poured upward and wrapped around the dog in slow, shifting ribbons, patterns folding inside patterns faster than Jeff's eyes could agree on, and the shape inside it stretched, and rose, and kept rising.

Alicia had the couch quilt airborne before the glow had even started to fade. It landed square on top of the new shape like a net dropped over a bird.

"Uh," said a voice from under the quilt. It was Biscuit's voice. Same kid. It just came out of a different throat now, a little lower, a little rougher at the edges, like a song played on a new instrument. "Why is it— oh. Oh. I have hands."

Two hands came out from under the quilt and held themselves up in front of the fabric. Then the quilt got pulled down to the shoulders, wrapped tight like a cape, and there was a boy standing on the rug. Fifteen, maybe. Tan skin tone. Very dark brown eyes. Wild, spiky blond hair that was, to the shade, the exact yellow of his coat.

He took one step, and his knee made a decision without consulting the rest of him. He caught the arm of the couch and hung there.

"Whoa. Whoa. Okay. Everything is really far down."

"The dog got taller," Yogi observed.

"How do you even— later, right, later." Jeff's grin was taking over his whole face. He couldn't stop it and wasn't trying.

He watched the boy test his own weight, and a quiet thought slid in under the noise. The wish hadn't made anyone new. There was no stranger in the living room. It was the same someone who'd been there the whole time, who argued about the playoffs and read the book on the side table and waited by the door. The wish had just handed him a different set of hands.

Biscuit let go of the couch. Stood. Swayed. Stayed up.

"Two legs is so much easier than I thought," he said, amazed. "You guys make it look hard. You guys are so dramatic about it." His hip twitched. He looked over his shoulder. His hip twitched again. "I keep trying to wag. There's nothing there. It's like reaching for a step that's not there."

"You'll get used to it," Alicia said.

"I don't want to get used to it, I liked it." Then his face changed, and Jeff saw the worry arrive all at once. Biscuit turned his head, closed his eyes, and breathed in through his nose. Long and slow.

His eyes snapped open.

"Yogi had eggs," he said. "Over easy."

Yogi was quiet for a moment. "That was three days ago."

A grin split Biscuit's face so wide it looked like it hurt. "Still got it." He turned toward the kitchen. "And there's a bag of chips open in the pantry that somebody said they didn't open."

"I have no idea what you're talking about," said Alicia, who had opened the chips.

"You could've wished me switchable." He looked down at his hands, then back at Jeff, and the grin didn't leave, but something rueful sat down next to it. "Human and dog. Whenever I wanted."

Jeff opened his mouth, closed it, and pointed at him.

"Yeah," he admitted. "That would've been the smarter wish."

"Okay," Alicia said, and clapped once, and the whole house seemed to change gears. "Weapons. Stuff. Now."

"Attic," Jeff said, already moving.

"Attic!"

"Do I need clothes?" Biscuit called, following them down the hall with the quilt dragging behind him like a royal train.

"YES," Jeff said. "Obviously. Stay away from me until you have pants."

"Whose pants?"

Jeff yanked down the attic ladder. Alicia was up it in four quick steps, her head and shoulders vanishing into the dusty dark, and then her voice drifted down, flat with realization.

"It's not up here."

"What do you mean it's not up here?"

"We moved it all after the raccoon thing."

"What raccoon thing?"

"The raccoon thing, Jeff."

Jeff closed his eyes briefly as the memory of the raccoon thing arrived. "Bedroom."

"Bedroom!"

Alicia came down the ladder fast and cracked the top of her head on the frame of the hatch on the way. It made a real sound, a solid wooden thunk.

"Huh," she said, mildly, and kept going.

Jeff's own scalp prickled in sympathy, a reflex for a kind of hurt she'd never have to collect on. He'd been married to her for years, and he still flinched every single time.

In the bedroom, Alicia dove into the closet and came out with an armful of red-and-white fabric, which she lobbed past Jeff into the hallway. "Biscuit! Those are Jeff's pajamas I shrunk in the dryer."

"That's where those went," Jeff said.

"They went to a better place."

"They were my good pajamas."

"And now they're his good pajamas."

From the hallway came the sounds of a boy fighting pajama pants for the first time in his life and losing several early rounds. Jeff pulled a hoodie out of his dresser and tossed it out the door without looking. "And that."

"This smells like you," Biscuit called.

"It's my hoodie."

"It's nice. It's just a lot."

Biscuit reappeared in the doorway wearing pajama pants that stopped a few inches above his ankles, a hoodie that went down to the middle of his thighs, and the fiercely dignified expression of someone who knows exactly how he looks and has decided to be above it. The drawstring hung loose. The waistband was already sliding.

Alicia crossed the room, knelt, and tied the drawstring in a firm double knot. "There. Now they'll stay up."

"Thanks." He peered down at the knot. "How'd you do that so fast? Fingers are hard."

"Practice."

Biscuit wiggled all ten of his and looked at them with deep suspicion.

The bag wasn't under the bed, so it was in the basement, and the basement was dark, because the bulb at the bottom of the stairs had been dead for a month and a half and both of them had decided, independently and without discussion, that it was the other one's job.

Halfway down the stairs, the tattoo on the back of Jeff's neck woke up.

It always did in the dark. A faint gold halo, glowing soft through the collar of his hoodie, and lower, behind the fabric over the right side of his stomach, the dim outline of a smiley face with a single red tear. Not bright. Just enough to see by if you didn't mind a little atmosphere.

"Your neck's on," Alicia said.

"I know my neck's on."

"I'm just saying, you're very easy to find down here."

"Find the bag."

She found the bag.

It was nothing to look at. A cloth sack the size of a pillowcase, with thin shoulder straps that had been repaired twice with thread that didn't match. Alicia shrugged it on, slid her whole arm into it up to the shoulder, and started naming things.

"Healing shards." Her hand came out holding a velvet pouch that clinked, and she tucked it into her hoodie pocket. Arm back in. "Coins. Rope." A coil of rope came out. She looked at it and put it back. "Rope." Another coil came out. "Why do we have so much rope?"

"You said rope is always useful."

"I did say that." She put the rope back. "Why did you listen to me."

Jeff dragged a heavy wooden box off the bottom shelf and flipped the lid. Crystal shards lay inside on old towels, broken pieces of a dozen different formations, each one and each of their auras faintly glowing its own color in the dark: amber, frost-blue, a sullen red, a green so bright it looked fake. He started filling his pockets, but he did it the way he'd learned to, holding each piece up against his own finger first. Anything shorter than that was just a very pretty paperweight. He knew because he had a drawer full of very pretty paperweights.

"Red," he muttered. "Blue. Blue. Too short. Too short. Why did I keep this one, it's a pebble."

"Sentimental value," Alicia said from inside the bag.

"It doesn't do anything."

"That's what makes it sentimental."

The sword and the staff lived in the shed, because Alicia had decided a long time ago that blades and ice didn't belong in the same house where people slept. They went out the back door at a dead sprint, across the yard, through the long grass, and Jeff wrenched the shed door open so hard it bounced off the wall and nearly closed on him again.

Alicia's staff hung on two pegs on the left wall: pale, smooth with a knot of blue-white crystal that breathed cold into the air around it. She took it down and spun it once, a quick, familiar flourish, and frost bloomed across the shed window in a thin fern pattern.

Jeff's sword hung on the right, in a plain black sheath.

He wrapped his hand around the grip, and the blade drank from him. It always did, first thing. A thread of warmth pulled out of his palm and up into the steel, patient and hungry, like something waking up and reaching for breakfast.

"Easy," Jeff told it, and buckled it on. "You'll eat later."

"You talk to your sword," Alicia said.

"You talk to your bag."

"My bag listens."

When they turned around, Biscuit was standing in the shed doorway in his too-short pajamas and his too-long hoodie, and he'd found shoes somewhere, and they were Alicia's.

"I'm coming," he said. "Right?"

"No," Jeff said.

"I have magic. And I have a nose. I can smell a dragon from—"

"Everyone can smell that dragon, bud. It's the size of a church."

"I'm fast."

"You were fast on four legs. Your two legs are about twelve minutes old." Jeff put a hand on top of the kid's head. He had to reach up to do it now, a little, and that was going to take some getting used to. "Stay here."

Biscuit stared at him.

"Did you just tell me to stay?"

Jeff heard it the same instant everyone else did.

"...In the human way," he said.

"Guard the house," Jeff said, recovering what little dignity he had left. "Watch the TV. If anything weird happens, remember it."

"Everything on that TV is weird."

"Then remember the weirdest stuff."

Biscuit looked like he wanted to keep arguing. Then his eyes went to Alicia, fully armed and practically vibrating, and to Jeff, whose irises were still blazing neon yellow, and something grown-up passed over his face for just a moment, and he let it go.

"I gotta show my friends," he said instead, a little wistfully. "They're not gonna believe this."

"IS IT STILL GOING?" Jeff yelled toward the house, forgetting for a second where Biscuit was standing.

Biscuit, directly in front of him, winced and put his hands over his ears. "Yes. And ow. It's still going."

They found Yogi in the backyard, standing in the sun beside the shed with his hands folded behind his back, exactly where nobody had left him.

Alicia hurried across the grass. "Can't Jeff just wish us over there?"

"Can't wish you anywhere," Jeff said. "Only me. It's a teleport."

"He's right," Biscuit said from the back steps. He had the book from the side table open in both hands, and he was turning the pages with his thumbs with an expression of pure, private joy. "'Any wish to teleport a conscious thinking existence is not able to be granted, except regarding teleporting oneself.' Page—" he turned another page just to do it again, "—dude. Pages are so easy with thumbs."

"So we do a portal," Alicia said.

"We do a portal."

Jeff turned to Yogi, and all at once the rush went out of him and something quieter came in behind it.

"This is the last one," he said.

"I know."

"You were a good genie."

"I was a great genie. I stood in the woods, flew upside down, and didn't hit anybody who didn't have it coming." Yogi tipped his head toward the house.

"Good luck, you guys," Yogi said, and meant it.

Jeff faced the empty patch of grass between the shed and the fence.

"Yogi. I wish for a magical portal to appear in my backyard that goes to that battle that's on TV."

Light gathered in the air beside the shed. It didn't crack or split. It built up in layers, like frost racing across a windowpane, until an oval taller than the shed door stood humming in the grass, glowing the hot, dusty orange of sandstone in afternoon sun. Through it, blurred like a city behind a shower door, Jeff could make out pillars and glare and, somewhere in the middle of it, movement much too big to be a person.

He turned to say something.

The grass where Yogi had been standing was already springing back up.

No flash. No goodbye. Just an old man in a cardigan, and then no old man in a cardigan. Jeff's pocket felt lighter, and when he patted it, it was empty. The cyan die was gone too, off to wherever those went when their work was done, somewhere so far away that the word "far" gave up trying.

"Bye, Yogi," Jeff said to the empty grass.

"I liked him," Biscuit said quietly from the steps.

"Yeah. Me too."

Alicia bounced on the balls of her feet, staff in hand, bag strapped tight. "Ready?"

Jeff looked at her. At the open glow of the portal humming by the shed that had been leaning toward their house for as long as they'd lived there. At the kid on the back steps, all long legs and borrowed shoes and hair the color of his old coat, holding the book in both hands like a trophy.

"Little bro," he said, pointing. "House."

"House," Biscuit agreed. "Bring me back a dragon tooth!"

"No promises!" Alicia called.

Jeff was grinning when he stepped through, leading with one leg, the way you ease into cold water.

Alicia went right behind him.

The portal hummed on in the backyard, patient and orange.

Biscuit went back inside, because the house was his job now.

The living room felt bigger with nobody in it. He sat down on the rug, and then, remembering he didn't have to, got up and sat on the couch like a person, and then slid back down onto the rug anyway because the rug was where he watched TV. Some things weren't about bodies.

On the screen, the canyon went on. The dragon's plates stood up in a new pattern. The armored man ducked a sheet of fire, rolled, came up, and slammed his fist against his chest plate twice.

Then, at the edge of the picture, near the canyon wall, a small orange glow blinked on, and two tiny figures stepped out of it.

"That's them," Biscuit said to nobody. "That's them."

He shuffled closer on his knees until his nose was a foot from the glass.

Biscuit's hip twitched, reaching for a tail that wasn't there.

Chapter 4: Joining the Battle

The air shimmered with heat waves rising from the sandstone formations that dominated the harsh landscape. Massive pillars of weathered rock jutted skyward like the fingers of buried titans, their surfaces carved smooth by wind and sand. The ground beneath was a mixture of compacted sand and exposed bedrock, cracked and broken in places where tectonic forces had reshaped the terrain.

Scattered across the canyon floor, far apart from one another, glass-like polygonal crystals rose out of the sandstone. No two formations resembled each other. One near the canyon's mouth stood taller than a man, tinted deep orange with darker orange twisting through its interior. Another, a hundred yards off, was barely knee-high, colorless and clear as ice, studded with small polygonal sub-growths. A third, alone atop a ridge in the distance, glowed faintly green from somewhere inside itself. They sat in the landscape like they had simply decided to be there.

Exiting the magical portal leg first, Jeff, then Alicia, stepped onto the terrain. The sound that hit them first was deafening—the roar of combat, the crash of magic against scale and stone, the ground-shaking impacts of a massive creature in battle.

Before them, perhaps two hundred yards distant, an enormous dragon dominated the battlefield. Its body was a fortress of armor plating, and its wing membranes were patterned in geometric whorls too regular to be natural. The dragon stood easily forty feet tall at the shoulder, its wingspan—when fully extended—capable of creating devastating windstorms.

Already engaged with the beast was the armored figure they'd seen on the broadcast. The warrior wielded a battle axe, its haft short enough to control one-handed, its twin blades a dull black that didn't match the gleam of his enchanted armor at all. As they watched, he slammed his gauntleted fist against his chest plate twice in rapid succession, then swept the axe forward in a wide arc. Lightning erupted from the axe's blades, striking multiple points along the dragon's flank with thunderous cracks. Without pause, the armored warrior planted his feet shoulder-width apart, raised the axe overhead with both hands, then brought it down in a chopping motion—not striking anything physically, but completing the gesture. His entire body converted into crackling electricity that shot forward, reforming beside the dragon's foreleg where his axe appeared mid-swing, crashing against the armor plating.

"We're doing this," Jeff said, his adrenaline rising. The irises of both his dark brown eyes turned glowing neon yellow, the pupils remaining black.

Alicia nodded, her hands already moving through the beginning gestures of a spell preparation, her fingers splayed and rotating in small circles at her sides.

Jeff performed his tongue simultaneous triple-fold. White feathered wings appeared attached to his back, the appendages flexing and spreading to their full span.

"Coordinated strikes on the wound points!" Alicia called out, already running toward a better vantage point, her eyes scanning the dragon's body for the spots where previous attacks had damaged its armor.

The dragon's attention snapped toward the newcomers, its head swiveling with terrifying speed. Along its neck and shoulders, armor plate flaps snapped open into standing positions, arranging themselves into a precise, unmistakable pattern—to perform magic. The creature reared back its head, then opened its massive jaws. Jeff's eyes widened as he saw the glow building in the back of its throat.

Jeff briefly locked his double-jointed pinky finger into a specific bent configuration. A powerful gust erupted around him, catching his wings and launching him skyward just as a torrent of blue flames scorched the ground where he'd been standing. He controlled the wind's direction using his left thumb like a joystick, guiding his aerial trajectory away from the flames. The fire didn't spray or billow the way fire should. It arrived in a disciplined column, the dragon corrected the aim mid-burn, and cut off its fire-attack the instant it missed—the economy of something that had made this exact attack countless times before.

The armored warrior took advantage of the dragon's focus on Jeff. He dropped to one knee, pressed the flat of the axe against the ground, then drew a quick symbol in the air with his free index finger—a jagged line representing lightning. Electrical energy poured out of the black blades and coursed through the earth, traveling up through the dragon's legs and causing its muscles to spasm momentarily.

Alicia reached the nearest crystal formation, a squat cluster of rose-tinted polygons jutting from the sandstone. No time to wonder what it did. She drew a large circle on it with her finger, placed both hands flat against its surface within the circle, fingers spread wide, and pushed hard on a facet angled toward the dragon.

Light poured from the crystal's opposite side—not a beam, not a blast, but a soft rose-colored radiance that unfurled through the air in slow, shimmering ribbons, curling and drifting like festival streamers. It washed over the sandstone, over the rocks, over the raging dragon, painting the entire battlefield a gentle, glowing pink.

Nothing burned. Nothing broke. It was, if anything, romantic.

"...Okay. Not you," Alicia told the crystal, already running.

The dragon tracked her movement through the rosy shimmer with one eye, and its plate flaps shifted into a new standing arrangement along its left shoulder. The air above Alicia condensed. Water—tons of it—materialized in a single suspended mass and dropped. She threw herself forward; the deluge hammered the ground behind her hard enough to crack stone, the flash flood chasing her heels through the fading pink glow.

"Watch its plates!" she shouted, rolling behind an outcropping.

Jeff soared upward, his wings beating in powerful strokes. Both his hands generated spheres of black energy courtesy of his cursed ring, each one pulsing with destructive potential. With immense throwing force, he launched the dark orbs directly at the dragon's eyes.

The dragon twisted with surprising agility for its size, one wing sweeping up to intercept. The dark orbs impacted against the geometric membrane, tearing twin holes through it in bursts of dissipating shadow. The dragon glanced at the damage dismissingly.
