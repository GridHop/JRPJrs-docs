    Personal identity consists in the continuity of one’s own mind as an ongoing causal and

Memory is a central component of that continuity because it carries much of a person’s accumulated

self-understanding. Memory is nonetheless not everything. A person can temporarily lack access to

memories without becoming numerically different, and losing a memory does not make the remembered

event cease to have happened or cease to have been part of that person’s history. What changes is

present access to part of that history. The view therefore has three interconnected layers:



• Mind continuity: the continuing individual mental process.

• Memory continuity: preservation of, and access to, much of the individual’s accumulated history.

• Causal continuity: the actual chain connecting earlier states of that mind to later ones.

18. Identity under presentism

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Under CTGP only the present exists, so the claim “I am the same person I was ten years ago” relates

something that exists (the present person) to something that does not (the earlier person). The same

resources that answer the general question about past truths answer this one. That the present

person was the earlier person is grounded in the present world’s past-tensed properties; the person

is the present stage of a process whose history is also physically written into it now, as memory,

neural structure, habits, dispositions, and commitments.



Memory is evidence of this continuity, not what creates it. A present brain is not merely a

container of information about its past; it is one of the physical consequences produced by that

past. The resulting self-description is not “I am the person who remembers being me” but rather “I

am the presently existing continuation produced through the causal development of that earlier

individual.”

19. Three kinds of continuity

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Three different continuities are easily run together:



• Physical continuity: a physical system continuously develops into its next state.

• Informational continuity: information about previous states remains encoded.

• Experiential continuity: successive conscious states belong to one stream of experience.



These can come apart. A hard drive can preserve informational continuity without experiential

continuity; a sleeping person can have reduced conscious activity while preserving physical and

informational continuity; and a perfect duplicate could share information and conscious organization

while still raising the question of numerical identity. CTGP’s extension to consciousness treats

causal continuity as one necessary condition for qualia-capable processes, while explicitly not

claiming to solve the hard problem of consciousness. In ordinary life the three continuities

coincide, which is why their distinctness is rarely noticed:



    persistence of organization ≠ persistence of information ≠ persistence of experience

20. Duplication: same information is not the same individual

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Imagine a technology that produces a second human with the same genome, brain structure, synaptic

strengths, relevant molecular states, memories, and personality. The duplicate wakes sincerely

believing “I am me, and I remember everything that happened.” There are now two physically distinct

causal continuations. A copied memory can preserve information about someone’s history without

transferring that person’s numerical identity:



    same information ≠ same history ≠ same individual



A history can be copied without the historical individual being copied. More precisely, a person’s

present state can be reproduced without the history that produced it being reproduced as the same

history. The duplicate is not thereby “fake.” It would be a fully real conscious individual whose

present state happens to encode another person’s history.



Care is needed about why the copy differs, because the copy’s memories are not causally disconnected

from the original events. The duplicate’s causal chain runs past event → original brain → scan →

construction → duplicate’s memory. Both chains are causal and both reach back to the event; what

differs is the route. One stays inside a continuously living organism; the other passes through

measurement, encoding, and reconstruction. Causal ancestry alone therefore does not settle identity.

A child is causally descended from a parent without being the parent, and a duplicate causally

descended from a brain state is not, by that fact alone, the person whose brain it was. Causal

dependence explains why something exists and how it arose; it does not by itself say whether the

result is the same thing as its cause. An identity criterion must specify which kinds of causal

pathway constitute continuation of an individual rather than production of a new one.

21. Branching shows that psychological continuity is not enough

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Suppose one person becomes two successors, each with the same memories, personality, and causal

ancestry up to the branching point. If psychological continuity were sufficient for identity, each

successor would be the original. But numerical identity is transitive: if A = B and A = C, then B =

C, and B and C are plainly two people. Therefore whatever relation preserves personal identity

cannot be unrestricted psychological continuity. This is the fission problem familiar from Derek

Parfit’s work, and it shows something structural: a relation can preserve everything that matters

psychologically while failing to preserve numerical identity.



The literary fiction work confronts this directly. In its fictional universe, a single continuing

existence can transition between different forms without being duplicated; non-duplication is a

world rule. That rule does real metaphysical work. It answers a question that psychological

continuity alone leaves open, namely what happens when continuity branches. In the actual world,

known physics does not rule out producing two successors that are alike in every physical and

psychological respect that plausibly matters (the no-cloning theorem forbids exact copies of unknown

quantum states, not copies at that level of detail), so a real-world continuity view needs an

explicit non-branching condition or an alternative criterion.



Presentism sharpens the problem in an instructive way. After a split, both successors carry

identical records of the pre-split history, and records can be copied. Under CTGP the fact of which

successor walked out of the scanner and which out of the printer is still grounded, because it

belongs to the present world’s past-tensed profile. What can be lost is access: if every

distinguishing record is duplicated or erased, the fact may become unrecoverable. The duplication

case is thus a clean example of a fact that exists without being accessible to the observers it most

concerns.

22. Transformation versus reconstruction

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Continuity of activity and continuity of identity are not the same thing. Temporary inactivity is

not necessarily discontinuity: during sleep, general anesthesia, or the deep hypothermic circulatory

arrest used in some surgery, during which the brain’s electrical activity can fall silent, the

brain’s organized physical structure remains intact, and later activity is causally continuous with

earlier activity through that persisting structure. “Uninterrupted electrical activity” therefore

cannot be the criterion.



Ordinary biological replacement preserves an ongoing causal organization: the existing organization

participates in determining how its own components are replaced, and at no stage does the person’s

complete state exist merely as information outside the person. Scan-and-rebuild procedures differ in

kind, because they insert a representation as an intermediate step. The original is measured, its

state is encoded, and another system is built from the encoding. If City A were converted into a map

and City B were then built from the map, no one would say that City A had traveled to B; something

informationally derived from A produced B. The same applies to persons: the information required to

reconstruct someone can survive without the individual whose information it is.



    Past → present through causal continuity: transformation. Past → representation → new

      instantiation: reconstruction.



One candidate criterion is therefore:



    Personal identity consists in the uninterrupted causal inheritance of an embodied organization,

    rather than in reconstruction from a representation.



If this criterion is adopted and applied consistently, it yields the following verdicts:



• Sleep, general anesthesia, and temporary cessation of detectable brain activity with structure

  intact: continuity.

• Gradual biological replacement incorporated into the existing organization: continuity.

• Gradual synthetic replacement incorporated in the same way: plausibly continuity, for the same

  reason.

• Scan, destruction of the original, and reconstruction elsewhere: discontinuity, meaning the death

  of the original and the creation of a new person. Many people find this conclusion hard to accept,

  and it is the crux of Parfit’s teletransporter case.

• Scan and reconstruction while the original survives: two individuals, not one becoming the other.

• A perfect copy made from stored information: a new individual, however psychologically identical.

• The Ship of Theseus repaired plank by plank: plausibly the same ship, since the existing

  organization continuously transforms rather than being rebuilt from a blueprint.



The criterion is not without difficulty. Consider neurons replaced one at a time by functional

equivalents until none of the originals remains. On a continuity reading, each replacement is

incorporated into an already existing organization. On a “micro-reconstruction” reading, each

replacement destroys one component and creates a successor from a description of it. Nothing in the

physical process announces which reading is correct. Between gradual molecular turnover and

whole-brain scan-and-rebuild lies a spectrum, and candidate dividing features include locality,

temporal continuity, preservation of causal organization, the absence of an informational

bottleneck, the absence of branching, or some combination. The framework has not established which

of these is decisive; what it has done is locate the precise unresolved variable. Whatever criterion

is chosen will settle the teleporter, the printer, gradual replacement, mind uploading, and the Ship

of Theseus at once.

23. Is the criterion discovered or chosen?

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Ordinarily, “we do not know whether X” means that there is a determinate fact not yet discovered.

With concepts such as numerical identity, it is possible instead that several coherent criteria give

different answers, and that no further physical measurement could decide between them, because the

measurement does not determine which criterion should govern the term. The 2006 decision of the

International Astronomical Union that reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet illustrates the

structure, though imperfectly, since “planet” is a classificatory term while personal identity

carries great metaphysical and practical weight.

tell us that X’s output is numerically identical to its input. The first is an empirical question;
