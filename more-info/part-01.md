# More Info — Causation, Information, Identity, and Becoming

Part 1 of 2

MORE INFO



Causation, Information, Identity, and Becoming



Philosophical consequences of Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism, a signal-based epistemology, and

the world rules of a literary fiction work





====================================================================================================



OVERVIEW



This document draws out the philosophical consequences that follow when three bodies of work are

read together. The first is Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism (CTGP), an ontology of spacetime in

which only the present exists, each present state is generated by the lawful evolution of the

preceding one, and the past survives only through its consequences. The second is A Signal-Based

Epistemology, a framework holding that all access to reality is mediated by detectable signals and

constructed through processes of signal generation, detection, and interpretation. The third is a

literary fiction work whose fictional universe has world rules that turn questions about

consciousness, identity, and justice into fixed features of the setting.



The connecting theme is a set of distinctions that are easy to collapse and costly to confuse:

reality versus descriptions of reality, causal influence versus surviving information, truth versus

recoverability, causation versus prediction, and numerical identity versus informational similarity.

Keeping these apart yields a picture of the world that is neither a prewritten script nor random

improvisation.



Two qualifications govern everything that follows. First, these are philosophical consequences, not

empirical results. CTGP itself acknowledges that presentist and eternalist readings of the same

physics are observationally equivalent, so its ontology cannot be confirmed by measurement at fixed

physics; it remains empirically exposed only through its requirement that the fundamental laws be

generable from the present state. Second, conclusions drawn from the literary fiction work concern a

fictional world. They are useful as thought experiments, not as evidence about how actual persons or

minds work.



====================================================================================================

PART I. Reality, Representation, and Knowledge

====================================================================================================



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Reality and information about reality are different categories

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



A record of something is not the thing itself. A photograph of yesterday is not yesterday, a memory

of an event is not the event, and a spacetime diagram containing an event is not the event

occurring. CTGP builds this distinction into its formalism: its generated domain M(σ) is a formal

representation of the present world’s history, laid out as if earlier stages coexisted so that

ordinary tensed reasoning can be applied, and not a collection of past states that continue to exist

alongside the present. The signal-based epistemology makes the parallel point about knowledge: the

past is reconstructed from present signals rather than directly observed.



The general principle is that a description can be structurally faithful without being ontologically

identical to what it describes. A map can accurately describe a city without containing the city,

and a mathematical model of a physical system is a formal structure that can correspond to physical

structure without being a second physical system sitting beside it. Three things must therefore be

kept distinct:



    referent ≠ representation ≠ instantiation



They can correspond without collapsing into one another.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2. Information is relational

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Consider a rock that is photographed. There is the rock; the electromagnetic interaction that

produces the image; the physical image; the information the image encodes; the observer’s

interpretation of that information; and the proposition the observer finally forms. Calling all six

“the information” blurs fundamentally different things.



Information is better understood as a relation between a physical state and what that state makes

distinguishable or representable. This is why the same information can have many physical

instantiations: the word “HELLO” can exist as ink, voltages, sound waves, magnetic domains, pixels,

or neural activity without being identical to any one of them. It also explains a useful asymmetry.

Destroying one representation of a piece of information does not destroy the information, but

destroying every physically available encoding may make it permanently inaccessible. Truth,

representation, and accessibility are three separate matters.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3. Knowing is not the same as having reality

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



The signal-based epistemology replaces the picture “thing → knowledge” with a mediated chain:



    thing → signal → detection → interpretation → knowledge



An observer does not simply possess reality. It receives some physically available information about

reality and constructs an understanding from it. Ignorance is therefore not necessarily a defect of

intelligence; sometimes reality itself imposes an information boundary. The framework identifies

logical, physical, and computational limits on knowledge, together with limits of detection and

interpretation, and it treats confirmation as a necessary condition for knowledge while recognizing

intrinsic limits on what can be verified. Two principles follow, and holding them together prevents

a large class of errors:



• A fact can exist without there being any possible observer capable of knowing it.

• A highly confident belief can exist without the corresponding fact being true.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4. Epistemic limits do not imply ontological vagueness

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Suppose an event occurs and leaves no surviving detectable trace. Two very different claims are then

available: “We do not know whether X happened,” and “There is no fact of the matter about whether X

happened.” The signal-based framework supports keeping these apart, because it ties knowledge to

signals, detection, and inference while allowing that some truths remain permanently inaccessible.

The boundary of knowledge is not necessarily the boundary of reality.



This safeguard cuts in both directions. It blocks the inference “I cannot detect X, therefore X does

not exist,” and it equally blocks “X could exist, therefore I am justified in believing X exists.”



It also shows that ignorance comes in kinds, not merely degrees. Three epistemic conditions should

be distinguished:



• Known: sufficient accessible evidence exists.

• Currently unknown: the evidence might become available.

• Structurally unknowable: the necessary information can never reach the relevant observer or

  system.



A person who does not know something because they have not looked is in a different condition from a

person who cannot know it because no possible signal could reach them. Ignorance can be temporary,

permanent, or principled.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5. One reality, many information channels

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Different observers possess different information because they differ in location, sensors, history,

computational capacity, causal access, and interpretive models. This does not require each observer

to inhabit a different reality. Observers can be differently connected to the same reality. The

resulting position is neither naive realism nor “everything is subjective”: it is one reality

accessed through many information channels. Disagreement between observers can then be explained by

differences in evidence, access, processing, or interpretation, without positing different

underlying worlds.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6. Coherence versus contact

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



The signal-based framework distinguishes internal coherence from external contact. Coherence shows

that a model hangs together; contact shows whether reality agrees with it. A perfectly coherent

model can still be wrong, because coherence is a property of the model alone, while contact requires

action and feedback from the world. The framework also characterizes good explanations as portable

“loop-packages”: structures that another mind can run, that preserve invariants, and that support

prediction and intervention.



On this view, knowing is not the possession of a complete picture of reality. It is the possession

of a representation that participates successfully in reality-directed loops of inference,

prediction, and action. Science is accordingly less like collecting photographs of the world and

more like building increasingly effective interfaces with it.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

7. Reality is intelligible because it exceeds our models

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



If reality were identical to whatever model an observer currently held, there would be no difference

between discovering something and constructing it. Because reality exceeds every observer’s

representation of it, reality can surprise the observer. That excess is what makes error, discovery,

science, and learning possible.



A related ladder shows how much is assumed at each step from contact to identity:



    causal access → information → representation → reconstruction → identity



Each arrow requires further conditions. One can detect a star without describing it fully, describe

it without reproducing it, and in principle reproduce the information in a brain without thereby

reproducing the person whose brain it was. Many conceptual mistakes consist in silently treating one

of these arrows as automatic.

PART II. Causation, Time, and Becoming

====================================================================================================



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

8. The present as the place where reality is generated

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



CTGP holds that reality exists only at an advancing present boundary. Earlier states do not persist

as independently real regions of spacetime; their specific physical organizations cease to exist as

present realities, while their causal consequences continue to propagate through later structures.

Present systems therefore inherit transformed physical continuities, causal records, and distributed

consequences of prior states, not the earlier states themselves.



The present is accordingly not merely “the point whose coordinate happens to be now.” It does

ontological work: it is the generated edge of reality. A memory of yesterday exists now, but

yesterday does not thereby become present again. Radiation arriving from a distant event exists now,

but the distant configuration that emitted it does not. The present can contain causal inheritance

from what no longer exists without containing the earlier state itself.



The past and the future are therefore radically asymmetric. The past can leave effects without

continuing to exist as past; the future can be causally possible without already existing as a

realized state. Under CTGP the future is not merely unknown but genuinely ungenerated.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

9. What makes truths about the past true

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Presentism faces a well-known question: if the past does not exist, what makes statements about it

true? CTGP adopts a Lucretian answer (after Bigelow, 1996). The truthmaker for a past-tense truth is

the present world itself, which instantiates primitive past-tensed properties of the form having

been, at stage σ′, such that p. These properties belong to the present state as a whole and, once

acquired, are retained at every later stage.



Records play a different role. Records are categorical physical structures that carry evidence about

the past, and they can be degraded or erased. They are evidence, not truthmakers. Losing a record

therefore does not change what happened; it changes only what an embedded observer can come to know.

CTGP states the cost of this position openly: past-tensed properties do not supervene on the

present’s categorical physical configuration, since two present states could agree in every

categorical respect while differing in their pasts. This is the familiar objection that Lucretian

properties “cheat” (Sider).



The distinction matters because it separates two questions that are easy to run together. Whether

something happened is settled by the present world’s past-tensed profile. Whether anyone can

establish that it happened depends on surviving records and on the signals available to an observer.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

10. Causal influence, surviving information, and recoverability

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Suppose A causes B, B causes C, and C causes D. By the time D exists, A may be entirely gone, and

there may be no recoverable record from which any observer could reconstruct it. D can nevertheless

be different because A occurred. Three notions must therefore be kept apart:



    causal influence ≠ surviving information ≠ recoverability



Information loss does not amount to causal loss. Burn a letter and its text may become

unrecoverable; but if someone read it first and made a decision because of it, the vanished

information has already altered subsequent reality. Eventually even the reader’s memory may fade

while the consequences of the decision continue to propagate. CTGP is consistent with this: it

entails that the universe’s accessible causal archive is finite and dynamically evolving, shaped by

record formation, thermodynamic degradation, and erasure, without implying that every past truth is

stored in a record.



In a metaphorical sense, reality retains the consequences of its past without containing a memory of

it. “Retains” here means is causally downstream of, not stores a representation of. Reality does not

represent; agents and other information-bearing systems do. The world can be truth-bearing, through

its past-tensed properties, without being representation-bearing.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

11. Everything causally active from the past is present now

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



CTGP’s most exposed empirical commitment is generability: the fundamental laws must fix the

transition from each present state to its successors (deterministically or probabilistically)

without reference to future boundary data, final-state constraints, or global self-consistency

conditions. CTGP adds that enlarging the state space to achieve this is legitimate only if the added

variables are physically instantiated on the present hypersurface and have dynamics of their own.



Combined with the doctrine of causal inheritance, generability yields a sharp principle:



    Whatever part of history can still affect the future must already be physically instantiated in

    the present.



Otherwise a past that no longer exists would have to reach forward and do additional causal work.

Two consequences follow. First, if two present states agreed in every physically instantiated

respect, their futures would be governed identically by the laws, whatever their histories; history

that left no physical trace in the present retains its truth, through past-tensed properties, but

has no further causal leverage. Second, “instantiated in the present” does not mean “decodable by an

observer.” Causal inheritance can be scrambled, distributed, thermodynamically degraded, or

computationally unrecoverable. Something can be causally preserved while being informationally

unrecoverable.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

12. Causality is lawful dependence, not foreknowledge

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



A cause does not need to “know” its effect. A neuron does not represent the decision the organism

will eventually make; a weather system does not contain a forecast of tomorrow’s weather; and under

CTGP the present does not need to contain a pre-existing future in order to generate one. A process

can produce a definite outcome without itself containing foreknowledge of that outcome.



    Causality is a relation of lawful dependence, not a relation of foreknowledge.



Several distinctions follow. Predictability and causation are separable: something can be causally

determined without being predictable by any observer, and something can be highly predictable

without the predictor being its cause. Likewise, a determinate outcome is not the same as a

pre-existing outcome. An event at t₂ may not exist at t₁, exist at t₂, and leave consequences at t₃,

without anything at t₁, including the universe itself, possessing a completed representation of t₂.



Three further concepts should not be conflated: prewritten, lawful, and predictable. Something can

be lawful without being prewritten, lawful without being computationally predictable by an observer,

and unpredictable without being causally unstructured. CTGP’s picture is precisely lawful generation

without prewritten specification: order does not require predetermination, and novelty does not

require causelessness.



Coherence can also be retrospective without being prescient. A completed causal process can be

reconstructed afterward as a continuous chain even though nothing within it possessed foreknowledge

of how it would end.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

13. A complete causal history without a complete history book

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Suppose the history from A to E was entirely lawful, and afterward all evidence distinguishing A

disappears. There can still be a fully definite answer to the question “Why is E this way?” while no

observer at E, and perhaps no possible later observer, can reconstruct that answer. The signal-based

epistemology explicitly allows such structural limits rather than treating all ignorance as a

temporary shortfall of technology. Hence:



• causal completeness does not entail epistemic completeness;

• intelligibility in principle does not entail reconstructibility in principle;

• reality can be lawful and determinate while being informationally lossy.



A further obstacle remains even where information has not been destroyed. Any observer attempting to

model the whole universe is itself part of the universe, so a complete model would have to include

the modeler and its own modeling activity, which raises problems of resources and self-reference.

The “view from nowhere” may therefore be structurally unavailable to any proper part of reality.

Being part of reality is itself an epistemic condition. In a phrase, reality can be fully

determinate while having thrown away the receipt.

PART III. Agency and the Representation of the Future

====================================================================================================



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

14. Agency does not require exemption from physics

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



CTGP describes conscious agency, where it exists, as an emergent causal process within lawful

physical dynamics rather than as an extra-physical selector of outcomes. This separates two ideas

that are often run together:



    agency ≠ exemption from causality



An agent can be physically constituted, causally influenced, internally structured, capable of

deliberation, capable of representing alternatives, and capable of changing its behavior for

reasons, and still be part of the causal chain. If an agent’s reasoning had no causal influence at

all, calling it an agent would be difficult. Causal efficacy is part of what makes agency

intelligible. A hurricane is caused by atmospheric conditions and is still a real process doing

things; a decision can be caused by prior conditions while the person’s reasoning, memories, values,

and actions are themselves links in the chain. “Caused” and “not really doing anything” are not

synonyms.



The signal-based framework makes a related point about creativity. The brain need not manufacture a

finished idea somewhere “in advance” and then merely reveal it to consciousness; creative

construction can occur through ongoing interaction, recombination, feedback, and modification in the

present. Causal systems can generate genuinely new configurations through their own dynamics.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

15. Memories and plans point in opposite causal directions

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Under CTGP, a memory and a plan are both present states about something that does not exist: a past

that is gone and a future not yet generated. They relate to their targets in opposite ways. A memory

is an effect of what it represents, since yesterday caused the memory of yesterday. A plan is a

cause, or at least a candidate cause, of what it represents, since a plan for tomorrow is among the

things that may generate tomorrow.



    A memory is true because of its causal ancestry. A plan becomes true, if it does, because of its

    causal descendants.



This yields a non-mystical definition of agency:



    An agent is a causal system whose present representations of possible future states participate

    in determining which future state actually occurs.



It also explains why prediction and intention are easily confused. A forecast and a plan can look

identical on paper, but they relate to the world differently:



• Prediction: present model → later correspondence. It succeeds by accurately describing what

  happens.

• Intention: present model → causal action → later correspondence. It can succeed partly because it

  existed.



A weather forecast predicts tomorrow; a human plan can help cause tomorrow. Nothing here involves

retrocausation. It is not the future affecting the present; it is a present representation of a

possible future affecting the present.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

16. Possibilities can matter causally without existing

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



An intelligent system can represent alternatives: “I could do A; I could do B; if A, probably C; if

B, probably D.” Neither imagined future needs to exist, but their present physical representations

do, and comparing them changes what happens next. Physically existing alternative futures are

therefore not required for alternatives to matter causally.



    possible future ≠ existing future, while a representation of a possible future is part of

    present causal reality



An open future does not imply an informationally empty present. The present can be full of models of

futures that do not exist, including plans, fears, simulations, forecasts, and contingency trees,

and those present representations help determine which later state occurs. Agency is one way reality

becomes causally responsive to possibilities without those possibilities being realities.



The present is thus an unusual junction. It contains causal inheritance from things that no longer

exist and representations of things that do not yet exist, and only the former came from its

referent:



• Memory: past event → present representation.

• Perception: present interaction → present representation.

• Intention: present representation → action → future event.



The same kind of thing, an information-bearing physical state, can play radically different causal

roles depending on its position in the causal network. A photograph of a fire and a plan to start a

fire may share descriptive content while doing opposite causal work.

PART IV. Personal Identity

====================================================================================================



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

17. Identity as the continuity of one’s own mind

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



A natural criterion of personal identity is the continuity of the individual’s own mind. This is

stronger and more precise than requiring the same body, atoms, or physical configuration, all of

which change over a lifetime: molecules are replaced, appearance changes, neural configuration

shifts from moment to moment, and memories are formed and lost. The relevant continuity is

continuity of the mind as an ongoing process, which makes identity fundamentally diachronic rather

than configurational. A snapshot of a person at 10:00 and another at 20:00 can be different physical

configurations while belonging to the same continuing individual.



“Mind” must mean more than the mental contents present at a given moment; otherwise sleep,

anesthesia, unconsciousness, or memory loss would seem to create a new person. A more robust

formulation is:



    Personal identity consists in the continuity of one’s own mind as an ongoing causal and

    psychological process, rather than in the persistence of any particular material configuration.



Memory is a central component of that continuity because it carries much of a person’s accumulated

history: experiences, relationships, knowledge, commitments, preferences, beliefs, decisions, and

self-understanding. Memory is nonetheless not everything. A person can temporarily lack access to

memories without becoming numerically different, and losing a memory does not make the remembered

event cease to have happened or cease to have been part of that person’s history. What changes is

present access to part of that history. The view therefore has three interconnected layers:



• Mind continuity: the continuing individual mental process.

• Memory continuity: preservation of, and access to, much of the individual’s accumulated history.

• Causal continuity: the actual chain connecting earlier states of that mind to later ones.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

18. Identity under presentism

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Under CTGP only the present exists, so the claim “I am the same person I was ten years ago” relates

something that exists (the present person) to something that does not (the earlier person). The same

resources that answer the general question about past truths answer this one. That the present

person was the earlier person is grounded in the present world’s past-tensed properties; the person

is the present stage of a process whose history is also physically written into it now, as memory,

neural structure, habits, dispositions, and commitments.



Memory is evidence of this continuity, not what creates it. A present brain is not merely a

container of information about its past; it is one of the physical consequences produced by that

past. The resulting self-description is not “I am the person who remembers being me” but rather “I

am the presently existing continuation produced through the causal development of that earlier

individual.”



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

19. Three kinds of continuity

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Three different continuities are easily run together:



• Physical continuity: a physical system continuously develops into its next state.

• Informational continuity: information about previous states remains encoded.

• Experiential continuity: successive conscious states belong to one stream of experience.



These can come apart. A hard drive can preserve informational continuity without experiential

continuity; a sleeping person can have reduced conscious activity while preserving physical and

informational continuity; and a perfect duplicate could share information and conscious organization

while still raising the question of numerical identity. CTGP’s extension to consciousness treats

causal continuity as one necessary condition for qualia-capable processes, while explicitly not

claiming to solve the hard problem of consciousness. In ordinary life the three continuities

coincide, which is why their distinctness is rarely noticed:



    persistence of organization ≠ persistence of information ≠ persistence of experience



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

20. Duplication: same information is not the same individual

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Imagine a technology that produces a second human with the same genome, brain structure, synaptic

strengths, relevant molecular states, memories, and personality. The duplicate wakes sincerely

believing “I am me, and I remember everything that happened.” There are now two physically distinct

causal continuations. A copied memory can preserve information about someone’s history without

transferring that person’s numerical identity:



    same information ≠ same history ≠ same individual



A history can be copied without the historical individual being copied. More precisely, a person’s

present state can be reproduced without the history that produced it being reproduced as the same

history. The duplicate is not thereby “fake.” It would be a fully real conscious individual whose

present state happens to encode another person’s history.



Care is needed about why the copy differs, because the copy’s memories are not causally disconnected

from the original events. The duplicate’s causal chain runs past event → original brain → scan →

construction → duplicate’s memory. Both chains are causal and both reach back to the event; what

differs is the route. One stays inside a continuously living organism; the other passes through

measurement, encoding, and reconstruction. Causal ancestry alone therefore does not settle identity.

A child is causally descended from a parent without being the parent, and a duplicate causally

descended from a brain state is not, by that fact alone, the person whose brain it was. Causal

dependence explains why something exists and how it arose; it does not by itself say whether the

result is the same thing as its cause. An identity criterion must specify which kinds of causal

pathway constitute continuation of an individual rather than production of a new one.



----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

21. Branching shows that psychological continuity is not enough

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------



Suppose one person becomes two successors, each with the same memories, personality, and causal

ancestry up to the branching point. If psychological continuity were sufficient for identity, each

successor would be the original. But numerical identity is transitive: if A = B and A = C, then B =

C, and B and C are plainly two people. Therefore whatever relation preserves personal identity

cannot be unrestricted psychological continuity. This is the fission problem familiar from Derek

Parfit’s work, and it shows something structural: a relation can preserve everything that matters

psychologically while failing to preserve numerical identity.
