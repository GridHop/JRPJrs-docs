There is also an important distinction between temporal order and causal order. Special relativity already tells us that the chronological order of spacelike-separated events can depend on the reference frame, while their causal relationship remains constrained by the light-cone structure. Quantum indefinite causal order is not simply another name for relativity of simultaneity.
And none of these results, by themselves, settles the philosophical debate between presentism and eternalism. A definite causal structure does not establish that only the present exists, just as an indefinite operational causal structure does not establish that past, present and future all exist equally.
One could also consider a generative view of time in which physical reality has one realized history, produced through successive stages, while certain quantum operations lack a definite ordering at the operational level. That is a possible interpretive program, not an experimentally established conclusion. It would need to specify the underlying dynamics and show that they reproduce the relevant quantum predictions.
This is close to the distinction between the physical state of a system and the truth of propositions about its history.
Suppose a particular event happened but left no recoverable record. The fact that nobody could reconstruct it would not logically imply that it never happened or that there was no fact about it.
Conversely, the mere logical possibility of such a hidden fact does not give us evidence that it exists.
Those two points are easy to confuse when discussing quantum mechanics.
The experiments tell us something definite about which operational descriptions fail. They do not automatically provide a complete ontology of time.
So my answer would be that the two alternatives in the question are not necessarily exhaustive, and they aren’t always mutually exclusive.
For the effective operations in a quantum switch, the process can be causally nonseparable. An ordinary classical mixture of definite operation orders is not an adequate explanation of its statistics.
For the finer-grained interactions in the laboratory, a definite spacetime causal ordering can still exist.
For a proposed deeper ordering of reality, the answer depends on the theory. Some possible underlying descriptions may preserve definite facts about trajectories or events; others may not. Whether those descriptions make distinct empirical predictions is a separate question.
And for genuinely quantum spacetime, we don’t yet have the experimental evidence needed to decide whether fundamental causal order must be indefinite.
What I wouldn’t conclude is that the absence of an operationally definite order proves there is no objective fact about any deeper ordering. Nor would I conclude that a definite underlying order must exist simply because ordinary experience presents events as happening successively.
The strongest conclusion currently justified is narrower but still remarkable: quantum theory permits experimentally supported processes whose effective causal ordering cannot be reduced to an ordinary classical choice between two definite orders, even though known laboratory realizations can be understood within a finer-grained, definitely ordered spacetime description.
Whether nature ever produces fundamentally indefinite spacetime causal order, and what that would mean for the ontology of time, remains open.
 
Does "experiencing backwards (T1→T0)"="one's experiences reverse-propagate, one is 'passenger of experience' "observing 'already present-in-own-brain info'" via the 'outcome' arrives before the 'prediction', making authorship/evaluation impossible"?
 
If "experiencing backwards (T1→T0)" means that conscious experience itself traverses an already-existing sequence of brain states in reverse order, then outcomes would indeed be encountered before predictions, conclusions before inferences, and answers before questions. In that sense, the experiencer would not be generating those outcomes within their experienced direction of time. They would encounter informational states that are already present in the brain state being traversed.
However, saying that the experiencer is "observing already-present-in-own-brain information" may be too strong. Observation, evaluation, interpretation, deduction, and judgment are themselves cognitive processes. Under a strict reversal, those processes would not be experienced as constructive activities. The subject would not first receive information and then actively work on it. Rather, each instant would simply contain whatever conscious (if the "experiencing backwards (T1→T0)" setup even enables being conscious) content belongs to that instant's brain state.
Likewise, saying that the outcome arrives before the prediction is broadly correct, but the reverse experiencer may not even experience these as an "outcome" and a "prediction." Those labels arise from relating multiple states across time. The reverse experiencer may lack access to the temporal organization needed to identify one state as the result of another.
For the same reason, authorship would not merely become difficult; the ordinary experience of authorship could disappear altogether. Authorship normally involves experiencing oneself as contributing to the production of later states. In a reversed experiential sequence, later states are encountered first. The subject would not experience themselves as bringing them about.
The same applies to evaluation. Evaluation is not simply hidden behind the outcome; evaluation itself is one of the states being traversed. The reverse experiencer would not necessarily experience evaluation as an activity occurring. They would merely pass through states that, from a forward-time description, correspond to stages of an evaluation process.
Thus, a strict T1→T0 experiential stream would not merely make authorship and evaluation impossible in the ordinary sense. It would undermine the very appearance of cognitive activity as activity. The experiencer would not primarily be a thinker who receives outcomes before predictions. Rather, they would be a conscious (if the "experiencing backwards (T1→T0)" setup even enables being conscious) succession of already-formed experiential states, lacking the temporal structure required for learning, inference, evaluation, authorship, or even the recognition that such processes are absent. The notions of "outcome," "prediction," "knowledge gain," "knowledge loss," "evaluation," and "authorship" would largely belong to an external description of the sequence rather than to what the experiencer could identify from within it.
 
Is there an alternative concept to the block universe that can account for the principle of simultaneity?
 
Yes. The block universe isn’t required to account for simultaneity. I think the important distinction is between what relativity establishes about simultaneous events, whether the universe admits a geometrically distinguished global time, and whether past, present, and future all physically exist. Those are different questions, and answering the first doesn’t automatically settle the others.
I’m interpreting the question as referring to the relativity of simultaneity: observers in relative motion can disagree about which spatially separated events occur at the same time.
In special relativity, the Lorentz transformation gives:
Δt′ = γ(Δt − vΔx/c²)
where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²).
If two spatially separated events are simultaneous in one inertial frame, Δt = 0, then another inertial frame moving relative to it generally assigns them different times:
Δt′ = −γvΔx/c²
That is an experimentally supported feature of relativistic spacetime. But it doesn’t mean simultaneity is meaningless, nor does it allow observers to disagree about the causal order of timelike- or lightlike-related events. It means distant simultaneity depends on the reference frame.
The argument that this favors the block universe is more interesting than simply saying that different observers disagree about “now.”
In the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, associated with Rietdijk (1966), Putnam (1967), and Penrose’s Andromeda example, the concern is that if every event simultaneous with me is equally real, and every inertial observer’s simultaneity relation has equal authority in determining what exists, then the differing simultaneity relations can be used to argue that events ordinarily classified as past or future must also be real.
Imagine two people walking past one another. Their relative motion gives them slightly different planes of distant simultaneity. At the distance of the Andromeda galaxy, even a small relative velocity can produce a substantial difference between which distant events each observer assigns to the same time as their encounter.
The disagreement is physical in the sense that the coordinate assignments follow from the Lorentz transformations. But the further claim that everything simultaneous with an observer must exist in the same ontological sense is not itself a Lorentz transformation or an experimentally established law.
The argument for eternalism therefore requires additional assumptions connecting simultaneity with existence. Those assumptions deserve consideration, but they aren’t automatically established by relativity.
There are several alternatives, although they don’t all preserve the same notion of a global present.
Howard Stein’s work on becoming in Minkowski spacetime offers one approach. Instead of requiring a universal simultaneity plane, becoming can be understood relative to individual spacetime events and their causal pasts. What has already become relative to an event is associated with its past light cone, including the event itself. This avoids requiring a global present shared by spatially separated events, although it also gives up the ordinary idea of a universal “now.”
Growing-block theories take another approach. They hold that past and present events exist while future events do not yet exist. C. D. Broad developed an influential version, while later proposals, including work by George Ellis and Tony Rothman, explore forms of relativistic or crystallizing becoming. Such approaches still need to explain how the boundary between what exists and what has not yet become is defined consistently with relativity.
Causal-set theory offers a different possibility. In the classical sequential-growth models developed by Rideout and Sorkin, a causal set grows by acquiring new elements. The auxiliary order in which causally unrelated elements are introduced has no independent physical significance; the causal structure is what matters. This provides a mathematical model of growth without requiring a physically preferred simultaneity slicing of continuous spacetime. It remains a research framework rather than an experimentally established account of fundamental spacetime.
Neo-Lorentzian approaches instead allow an underlying preferred frame or foliation while preserving the observable predictions of special relativity. Such a structure is not required by the established experiments, but the relativity of simultaneity alone doesn’t logically exclude every interpretation containing an empirically hidden preferred frame.
There’s also an important possibility within general relativity itself: the fundamental laws may not select a universal preferred foliation, while a particular cosmological spacetime can possess geometrical structure that distinguishes one.
In an idealized FLRW universe, cosmic time is the proper time measured along the worldlines of comoving observers. Events assigned the same cosmic-time value belong to the same cosmological time slice. Other observers can use different coordinates and different simultaneity conventions without invalidating that geometrical description.
The cosmic microwave background gives us an observable cosmological rest-frame reference. Its near-isotropy can be used to identify motion relative to the large-scale cosmological matter-radiation distribution. But that measurable frame should not automatically be identified with a uniquely existing present, nor assumed to coincide exactly with every proposed cosmological-time slicing in an inhomogeneous universe.
A more general construction is the cosmological time function studied by Lars Andersson, Gregory Galloway, and Ralph Howard.
It can be written as:
τ(p) = sup { L(γ) : γ is a past-directed causal curve starting at p }
