This does not mean information loss is always causally irrelevant. Erasing information can change a system’s subsequent behavior. The point is that losing evidence of an earlier cause does not retroactively undo its effects.

Under CTGP, present physical reality may bear consequences of earlier events without containing enough information to reconstruct those events.

A present consequence is not necessarily a record of its cause.

11. The present physical basis of future causation

CTGP’s generability commitment requires the fundamental laws to determine, deterministically or probabilistically, the transition from the present state to subsequent states without independently imposed future boundary conditions, final-state conditions, or global consistency requirements spanning the whole history.

CTGP further restricts attempts to enlarge the state space: additional dynamical variables must be physically instantiated in the present and possess appropriate dynamics.

A central principle follows within the framework:

Whatever present or future effects depend on earlier events must be generated through physically instantiated present conditions and the lawful dynamics acting on them.

Earlier states cannot perform new causal work after ceasing to exist.

If two present states agree in every categorical physical respect, CTGP requires what comes next for each to be governed by the same laws and transition probabilities, regardless of differences in past-tensed truth profiles that have no dynamical role.

This does not require observers to decode every causally relevant feature. Present physical conditions may be distributed, complex, scrambled, or computationally inaccessible.

Physical causal relevance is not identical to recoverable historical information.

This is a theoretical commitment of CTGP, not an independently established result about every possible fundamental theory.

12. Causality is lawful dependence, not foreknowledge

A cause need not know its effect.

A neuron need not represent the decision in which it participates. A weather system need not contain a forecast of tomorrow’s weather. Under CTGP, the present need not contain an already existing future to generate subsequent states.

Causality is lawful dependence, not foreknowledge.

Predictability and causation are distinct. A process can be causally determined yet computationally unpredictable, while a reliable prediction does not necessarily cause what it predicts.

Likewise, an outcome’s definiteness when realized does not establish that it existed before its realization.

Three concepts must be distinguished:

prewritten ≠ lawful ≠ predictable

Lawfulness does not necessarily imply that outcomes are predetermined, and unpredictability does not imply absence of causal structure.

CTGP allows lawful generation without an already existing future. Whether a particular generative law is deterministic or stochastic is a separate question.

A completed causal sequence can be reconstructed retrospectively even when no earlier participant possessed a complete representation of its outcome.

13. A complete causal history without a complete history book

Suppose a lawful sequence runs from A to E, but afterward all evidence distinguishing A from alternative antecedents disappears.

There may still be a determinate fact about what happened, even if no observer can reconstruct it.

The signal-based epistemology allows this distinction:

\* Causal completeness does not entail epistemic completeness.  
\* Intelligibility does not guarantee reconstructibility.  
\* A lawful reality can be informationally lossy.

Even where information survives, a further limitation arises: an observer modeling the universe is itself part of the universe.

A perfectly complete internal model would face demanding requirements involving physical resources, self-reference, and representation of its own modeling activity. These create obstacles to obtaining a complete “view from nowhere,” although they do not alone prove that every conceivable form of complete knowledge is impossible.

Reality can be determinate without furnishing observers with a complete recoverable account of its history.

PART III. Agency and the Representation of the Future

14. Agency does not require exemption from physics

CTGP can accommodate conscious agency, where it exists, as a physically instantiated causal process rather than an extra-physical power that selects outcomes independently of physical laws.

Thus:

agency ≠ exemption from causality

An agent can be physically constituted, influenced by earlier conditions, capable of deliberation, responsive to reasons, and causally effective.

Reasoning, memories, values, emotions, interpretations, and decisions can participate in the processes producing behavior. Being causally influenced does not mean one’s reasoning has no causal role.

The signal-based framework similarly allows creative thought to develop through ongoing recombination, evaluation, feedback, and modification rather than through the revelation of a fully formed idea existing beforehand.

New configurations can arise through lawful physical processes.

15. Memories and plans have different causal roles

Under CTGP, memories and plans are presently existing physical and mental states representing events at other times.

Their causal relationships differ.

A memory ordinarily arises through an appropriate causal connection with an earlier experience. Its accuracy depends on correspondence with what happened, while its status as a genuine memory also depends on the relevant connection between the experience and the present memory.

A plan represents a possible future and may help bring about what it represents.

Thus:

\* Memory: earlier experience → present representation.  
\* Prediction: present representation → possible later correspondence.  
\* Intention: present representation → action → possible later outcome.

A forecast succeeds by accurately describing what occurs. An intention can succeed partly because it influences what occurs.

This suggests a functional description of agency:

An agent is a causal system whose representations of possible future states can participate in determining its subsequent behavior and outcomes.

Nothing requires future events to exist already. A presently existing representation of a possible future can have causal effects without the represented future being real.

16. Representing alternatives does not require existing alternative futures

An intelligent system can consider alternatives:

“I could do A or B. If I do A, C may follow; if I do B, D may follow.”

The represented alternatives exist as present informational or cognitive states. The alternative future events themselves need not exist.

Therefore:

possible future ≠ existing future

Yet a representation of a possible future can be part of present causal reality.

Plans, fears, forecasts, simulations, and contingency models can influence behavior. This permits agents to respond causally to possibilities without those possibilities already being actual.

The same general kind of physical structure—an information-bearing representation—can have different causal roles depending on its origin and effects.

A photograph of a fire and a plan to start a fire may represent similar events while functioning very differently.

PART IV. Personal Identity

17. Identity as continuity of one’s own mind

One candidate account of personal identity emphasizes the continuity of an individual’s mind as an ongoing causal and psychological process.

This does not require retaining the same atoms, bodily configuration, or mental contents throughout life.

Molecules are replaced, memories change, and neural activity varies. A person can undergo substantial physical and psychological alteration while remaining the same individual under ordinary identity judgments.

The proposed criterion is:

Personal identity consists in the continuity of one’s own mind as an ongoing causal and psychological process, rather than persistence of a particular material configuration.

Here, “mind” cannot mean only the thoughts currently experienced. Sleep, anesthesia, temporary unconsciousness, and memory loss need not terminate personal identity.

Three forms of continuity are relevant:

\* Mental continuity: persistence of the individual’s organized psychological capacities and processes.  
\* Memory continuity: preservation of and access to aspects of accumulated experience.  
\* Causal continuity: the actual physical and psychological development connecting earlier and later stages.

These relationships are important, but their precise sufficiency for numerical identity remains philosophically contested.

18. Identity under presentism

Under CTGP, a person can persist while their earlier states do not: what no longer exists is the state they were in ten years ago, not necessarily the person. Saying “I am the person I was ten years ago” claims that the presently existing individual is the one who was in that earlier state.

CTGP grounds the relevant past-tense truth in the present world’s primitive past-tensed properties.

Present memories, neural organization, habits, dispositions, and commitments may also provide physical evidence of causal continuity.

Memory does not create the historical fact of identity. Nor does remembering an event automatically establish that the rememberer personally experienced it.

The proposed account emphasizes that the person’s present state is a causal continuation of their earlier states, while recognizing that causal continuation alone does not settle every question about numerical identity.

19. Three kinds of persistence

persistence of organization ≠ persistence of information ≠ persistence of experience

An organized system may persist while its constituent materials change.

Information may survive the destruction of its original encoding if another encoding exists.

An experience may cease even though information describing it remains available.

These distinctions become especially important in hypothetical cases involving copying, reconstruction, and consciousness.

20. Duplication: information is not numerical identity

Imagine producing a second human with the original person’s relevant brain structure, memories, personality, and cognitive dispositions.

The duplicate sincerely believes it has lived the original person’s life.

Nevertheless, the two are physically distinct individuals after duplication.

Thus:

same information ≠ same causal history ≠ numerical identity

The duplicate’s memories are not necessarily causally disconnected from the original experiences. Their causal ancestry may run through the original brain, scanning equipment, stored information, and reconstruction.

But causal ancestry alone does not establish numerical identity. A child is causally descended from a parent without being that parent.

An identity theory must specify which causal relationships constitute continuation of an individual rather than production of another.

The duplicate need not be considered unreal or inferior. If conscious, it would be a real individual whose psychological state includes information derived from another person’s experiences.

21. Branching and psychological continuity

Suppose one person produces two psychologically continuous successors, each possessing the original memories and personality.

If psychological continuity alone guaranteed numerical identity, both successors would be identical to the original. By transitivity, they would then be identical to each other, despite being two distinct individuals.

This is the fission problem associated with Derek Parfit.

It demonstrates that unrestricted psychological continuity cannot, by itself, serve as a sufficient criterion of numerical identity.

The quantum no-cloning theorem prohibits perfect copying of arbitrary unknown quantum states. It does not, by itself, establish that producing functionally or psychologically similar successors is impossible.

Whether such duplication could ever be technologically achieved remains unknown.

Under CTGP, each successor would possess its own present physical state and causal history. Records might establish which successor underwent which procedure, but the numerical-identity question still requires a theory of persistence.

Even if every distinguishing record disappeared, that would not automatically erase the historical facts.

22. Transformation versus reconstruction

Continuity of activity is not identical to continuity of identity.

During sleep or anesthesia, conscious experience may be absent or altered while the brain’s organized physical structure remains. Temporary suppression of detectable neural activity need not imply destruction of the individual.

Ordinary biological replacement involves ongoing physical organization participating in its own maintenance.

Scan-and-rebuild procedures introduce a different causal pathway: a system is measured, information is encoded, and another system is constructed from that representation.
