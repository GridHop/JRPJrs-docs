This is the fission problem associated with Derek Parfit.

It demonstrates that unrestricted psychological continuity cannot, by itself, serve as a sufficient criterion of numerical identity.

The quantum no-cloning theorem prohibits perfect copying of arbitrary unknown quantum states. It does not, by itself, establish that producing functionally or psychologically similar successors is impossible.

Whether such duplication could ever be technologically achieved remains unknown.

Under CTGP, each successor would possess its own present physical state and causal history. Records might establish which successor underwent which procedure, but the numerical-identity question still requires a theory of persistence.

Even if every distinguishing record disappeared, that would not automatically erase the historical facts.

22. Transformation versus reconstruction

Continuity of activity is not identical to continuity of identity.

During sleep or anesthesia, conscious experience may be absent or altered while the brain’s organized physical structure remains. Temporary suppression of detectable neural activity need not imply destruction of the individual.

Ordinary biological replacement involves ongoing physical organization participating in its own maintenance.

Scan-and-rebuild procedures introduce a different causal pathway: a system is measured, information is encoded, and another system is constructed from that representation.

Consider a city reconstructed elsewhere from detailed plans. The new city may closely resemble the original without thereby being numerically identical to it.

Similarly, reconstructing someone’s physical and psychological organization does not automatically establish preservation of that person’s numerical identity.

The distinction motivates a candidate criterion:

Personal identity consists in uninterrupted causal inheritance of an embodied organization, rather than reconstruction from a representation.

This is a philosophical proposal, not an established theorem of physics.

If adopted, it suggests:

\* Sleep and anesthesia with continuing physical organization: identity preserved.  
\* Gradual biological replacement within an ongoing organism: identity preserved.  
\* Gradual synthetic replacement integrated into that organization: plausibly preserved.  
\* Scan, destruction, and reconstruction elsewhere: identity not preserved under this criterion.  
\* Reconstruction while the original survives: two distinct individuals.  
\* Copying from stored information: creation of another individual.  
\* Gradual repair of the Ship of Theseus: plausibly preservation of the original ship.

Difficult cases remain. Replacing neurons individually with functional equivalents could be understood as continuing an organization or as successive local reconstructions.

Potentially relevant features include locality, temporal continuity, preservation of causal organization, informational bottlenecks, and branching.

The framework has not established which combination is decisive. It identifies the unresolved identity conditions.

23. Is the identity criterion discovered or chosen?

Some disagreements concern unknown empirical facts. Others concern which criteria should determine the application of a concept.

Personal identity may involve both.

Physics could describe a reconstruction procedure in extraordinary detail without independently settling whether its result counts as the same person.

That depends partly on the theory of persistence being applied.

This does not prove that identity is merely conventional. It leaves open whether reality determines a unique identity relation or whether competing criteria capture different legitimate concerns.

The central conclusion is:

Preserving information about an individual does not, by itself, establish preservation of that individual’s numerical identity.

A theory of identity must explain what kind of continuity, if any, makes a later individual the same person.

PART V. Human Duplication as a Future Technology

24. Clones and duplicates

A biological clone is primarily a genetic copy, not a copy of an existing person’s memories and personality.

A cloned human, if reproductive cloning became feasible, would ordinarily develop from an embryo rather than emerge as an adult duplicate.

Mammalian cloning has been demonstrated since Dolly the sheep’s birth in 1996, and cloned macaques were reported in 2018.

Human reproductive cloning is not an established technology and raises substantial safety and ethical concerns.

A duplicate possessing someone’s detailed physical and mental organization would be a much more demanding achievement.

25. Why full duplication remains remote

The human brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons and an estimated hundreds of trillions of synaptic connections, depending on counting methods.

Reconstructing a person’s memories and cognitive organization would likely require much more than a simple wiring diagram, including relevant synaptic strengths, molecular states, and other physiological features.

A complete adult fruit-fly brain connectome, involving roughly 140,000 neurons and about 50 million synapses, was published in 2024 after extensive collaborative work.

Current high-resolution connectomic methods generally require invasive tissue preparation and are not suitable for nondestructively scanning a living human brain at comparable detail.

A 2025 survey of 312 neuroscientists reported substantial support for the role of neuronal connectivity and synaptic strengths in long-term memory, alongside disagreement about which details are essential.

Its median estimate for the possible arrival of human whole-brain emulation was approximately 2125. This represents surveyed expectations, not a validated technological forecast.

26. Degrees of technological plausibility

Different kinds of copying require different capabilities.

\* Genetically similar humans: biologically plausible in principle; human reproductive cloning remains unestablished and unsafe.  
\* Engineered biological traits: active areas of research, subject to major technical and ethical constraints.  
\* Detailed human brain-structure copying: far beyond current capabilities.  
\* Reproducing memories and personality in another brain: unknown feasibility.  
\* Whole-brain emulation: an active speculative research goal, not achieved for a complete human mind.  
\* Exact instantaneous physical-state copying: subject to severe measurement constraints, including quantum restrictions.  
\* Preserving original numerical identity: not merely an engineering specification; it also requires philosophical analysis.

Even if two individuals began with nearly identical relevant states, their experiences and physical development would ordinarily diverge as they interacted with different surroundings.

Successful copying would not automatically settle whether the result was the original individual or a newly created one.

27. What would need to be copied

A functional duplicate would not necessarily require reproducing every photon, field fluctuation, or environmental detail associated with a person.

The relevant target would be the physical organization sufficient to reproduce the intended biological, cognitive, and psychological functions.

Exactly which details are necessary remains unknown.

Brain–computer interfaces can already decode restricted forms of neural activity for communication or device control, but they are far from extracting and reconstructing a complete human mind.

Functional equivalence, conscious experience, and numerical identity are separate questions.

28. The unaware duplicate

A hypothetical duplicate possessing the original’s memories might sincerely believe it personally experienced every remembered event.

Its internal evidence could be indistinguishable from that of the original, depending on how completely the relevant cognitive states were reproduced.

That would not independently establish the historical identity of the duplicate.

External evidence about the duplication process might distinguish their causal histories.

This separates two claims:

“I remember X” and “I personally experienced X.”

The first does not logically guarantee the second.

29. Social and legal consequences

Human duplication, if technologically possible, would raise difficult questions concerning identity, consent, property, relationships, contracts, citizenship, employment, and responsibility.

Both individuals might sincerely claim the same autobiographical history and associated relationships.

A reasonable ethical starting point is to recognize each conscious duplicate as an individual deserving appropriate moral consideration, rather than treating one as less real because of its origin.

The legal allocation of rights and responsibilities would require new rules.

PART VI. Parts and Wholes

30. Being in the universe is not being the universe

The relation “is part of” must be distinguished from “is identical to.”

A teapot in a kitchen occupies part of a house and interacts with its surroundings, but it is not the house.

Likewise, humans are physically constituted within the universe, depend on its processes, and interact with other parts of it.

That does not make any individual human numerically identical to the universe as a whole.

Being part of reality does not mean being identical to all of reality.

Can “agony from fire-burn” be only via the literal sensory experience, while/and not via a “reasoning, psychological, evaluation-related, emotion-related, mood-related, nor voluntary” thing? Can “agony from fire-burn” be only a “physical pain”-“subjective experience”?

If an entity can undergo subjective experience but doesn’t have “capacity/capability to analyze/evaluate” to even be able to have an opinion about it, is that entity capable of suffering? Can apples/mushrooms undergo subjective experience when eaten?

With “interaction” meaning signals being made available, received, and/or acted upon within “the universe as a whole”, can whole-universe states’ things/entities physically directly interact from distinct states, not just only be causally connected?

When it comes to experimentally established physics and mathematical descriptions that successfully account for observations, no direct physical interaction between entities as instantiated in distinct whole-universe states has been demonstrated, established, and/or required by the evidence. That doesn’t mean such interaction has been proved impossible under every conceivable physical theory. There’s an important difference between what established physics describes, what a particular theory excludes by construction, and what we can conclude about the universe itself.

I think the first thing to distinguish is physical interaction from causal connection. By direct physical interaction, I mean a physical coupling between specified entities, rather than merely a causal relationship established through intermediate physical carriers or processes. I’m not assuming in advance that direct coupling must occur at a single spacetime event, because that would partly settle the question by definition.

Take light emitted by a distant star and subsequently detected here. The detector interacts with the electromagnetic field physically available at the detection event. The star’s earlier emission is causally relevant, but that doesn’t establish that the detector directly interacts with the star as it was at the earlier stage. In the standard description, the field mediates the relationship. The detector doesn’t need the star’s earlier configuration to exist alongside it for the detection to occur.

This also matters for what I mean by signals being made available, received, and/or acted upon. Making a signal available doesn’t automatically mean interacting with some particular future recipient, and receiving one doesn’t automatically mean directly interacting with its original source. Those are physical activities involving signals, but whether two specified entities directly interact is a further question. Requiring all three activities, or allowing any one of them, doesn’t change that distinction.

In standard local relativistic quantum field theory, the locality of interactions is part of the mathematical structure. Interaction terms are built from fields and, where applicable, their derivatives evaluated at the same spacetime point. A simple example is:

L_int(x) = g φ₁(x) φ₂(x) φ₃(x)

The fields in that expression are coupled at x, not through a fundamental interaction term directly joining independently specified events at different times.

There’s also microcausality, which for suitable local observables requires:

[A(x), B(y)] = 0

when x and y are spacelike separated. But that shouldn’t be confused with a general prohibition on every conceivable direct interaction between different temporal stages. Events on different hypersurfaces of a foliation can be spacelike, lightlike, or timelike related, and microcausality concerns only the spacelike case; the locality of the interaction terms is the more relevant point here.
