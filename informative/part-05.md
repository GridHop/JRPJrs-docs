The conclusion I think the evidence supports is that local physical interactions and causal connections across time are established, whereas direct physical interaction between entities as instantiated in distinct whole-universe states has not been demonstrated, established, nor required by the evidence. Standard local relativistic field theories account for the relevant observations without requiring such cross-stage direct coupling. Alternative formulations show why universal impossibility shouldn’t simply be assumed, but they don’t establish that the universe actually contains independently interacting global temporal states.

In other words, being causally connected across different times isn’t the same as directly interacting from those different times, and neither relationship by itself tells us whether past, present, and future coexist.

According to general relativity, could someone light-years away take only 5 seconds of their own time to speak a sentence while years pass here before they finish speaking, even though both locations exist in the same universe progressing through time?

Established relativistic physics: Different worldlines (the paths objects take through spacetime) can accumulate dramatically different amounts of proper time between appropriately specified events. Someone could experience only 5 seconds while speaking a sentence, whereas years elapse on another person’s clock over a corresponding interval. Distance alone doesn’t cause this; relativistic motion and gravitational conditions can.

Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism (CTGP), adds an ontological interpretation: Only the currently generated state of the universe exists. Earlier states no longer exist as states, and future states have not yet been generated.

CTGP identifies the present with a cosmological-time hypersurface: a three-dimensional spatial slice of spacetime whose points share the same value of cosmological time. Cosmological time is defined as the supremum of proper time along past-directed causal curves from each event, roughly corresponding, in an idealized expanding universe, to the greatest elapsed proper time since the Big Bang.

Where cosmological time is regular, these hypersurfaces are future Cauchy surfaces: every worldline that continues past one crosses it exactly once. CTGP interprets each such surface as one objectively existing present, making all its locations co-present, even when separated by light-years.

As successive hypersurfaces are generated, different observers can accumulate very different amounts of local proper time. Neither observer’s clock determines the progression of the shared present.

In short: Relativity permits different elapsed local times; CTGP interprets the corresponding events as belonging to one succession of objectively generated presents. The latter is a philosophical interpretation, not something general relativity independently establishes.

Agency survives under determinism because the relevant issue is not exemption from causation but whether action flows through the organism’s internally integrated evaluative architecture. Randomness alone does not generate authorship. Higher-level causation is able to be real in a block. Hurricane analogy and interventionist account of causation do not require an open future. A block contains organizational structures (hurricanes, brains, evaluative architectures) as real patterns. Interventions at the level of intentions reliably change behavior; that counterfactual structure is available in a block as relations among possible histories. In a block, the agent did not create its own architecture ex nihilo; it performs the relevant computation, evaluation, prediction, and behavioral integration at its own time. Predictive processing does not require an unsettled future. In a block, the brain’s forward models are present-state structures that bias subsequent processing. Those models and their alignment with outcomes are part of the fixed history. The organism is not “predicting a future that could be otherwise”; it is generating anticipatory states that causally guide behavior. The felt sense of control arises when predictions and outcomes align, whether the block is deterministic or stochastic. “Decisions unfold only in the present moment” is indexical, not ontological. Each decision event is present to the agent at its own time. A block can say that. Accountability is grounded in the agent’s evaluative architecture, responsiveness to reasons, and causal role in shaping outcomes. That makes accountability compatible with a block. Justification shifts from libertarian desert to causal agency, answerability, responsiveness to reasons, and forward-looking social practices.

Things can sometimes be identified at the macroscopic level by their organization, continuity, or identity-defining characteristics, rather than by every individual constituent at lower scales. This means they don’t necessarily need to retain every single characteristic to remain the same entity.

Therefore, a thing can remain what it is as long as the characteristics essential to its continued identity are preserved, but that doesn’t mean the thing hasn’t changed in other respects.

For example, a hurricane can be understood as a persisting macroscopic pattern or organized physical system, even while its constituent molecules, location, intensity, and structure change.

Likewise, an organism can remain the same individual while undergoing physical changes.

So I’d distinguish an entity remaining numerically the same from remaining qualitatively unchanged. An entity doesn’t necessarily need to retain every characteristic to remain itself; only whatever conditions are necessary for its continued identity.

Regarding the philosophical distinction between essential and nonessential properties, some properties may be necessary for an entity to remain what it is, while others can change without terminating its identity.

For example, a human can change height, location, age, and cellular composition without necessarily ceasing to be the same individual.

However, I’d distinguish the properties necessary for being a particular kind of entity from the conditions necessary for remaining the same individual. Those conditions may also involve causal or historical continuity, rather than just an unchanging set of properties.

So I’m not claiming that every entity necessarily has a universally agreed-upon division of properties. I’m saying that changing a property doesn’t automatically entail losing numerical identity.

Can a physical theory in principle derive a definite underlying event history while allowing some effective quantum operations to lack a definite order or allowing operational descriptions that assign no single classical ordering to those operations?

Yes, in principle, in suitable theoretical settings. A definite underlying event history and an indefinite operational causal order are not necessarily contradictory. The crucial distinction is between the physical events that actually occur and the effective quantum operations used to describe an experiment.
In nonrelativistic settings, single-outcome theories such as Bohmian mechanics and GRW-type collapse theories already derive definite trajectories or events from their dynamics while retaining quantum interference (section 3). However, these examples do not, by themselves, establish a complete relativistic theory deriving a single realized history for all operational indefinite-causal-order phenomena.
1. What indefinite causal order (ICO) experiments establish
In a quantum switch, two operations, A and B, can be coherently controlled so that the experiment cannot generally be represented as an ordinary probabilistic mixture of the fixed orders A-before-B and B-before-A.
Interference between these alternatives can produce operational statistics incompatible with such a mixture. This is evidence of causal nonseparability at the level of the effective operations.
However, causal nonseparability does not, by itself, establish that every underlying physical event lacks a definite causal relationship or that spacetime itself has no definite causal structure.
Also, the standard quantum switch does not, by itself, violate a causal inequality. Causal nonseparability and causal-inequality violation are distinct properties.
Recent device-independent tests certify the switch's indefinite order in a different way: they add a spacelike-separated party entangled with the switch and assume relativistic causality and free interventions, a Bell-type scenario rather than a standard causal inequality (van der Lugt, Barrett, and Chiribella 2023). Photonic experiments along these lines have been reported (Richter et al. 2026; Qu et al. 2025), with loopholes still open.
2. How a definite underlying history can coexist with operational ICO
Consider a quantum-switch experiment implemented using a coherent superposition of different paths through physical devices.
At the effective level, operations A and B have no single classical order that adequately describes the process.
At the more detailed physical level, however, the apparatus consists of interactions occurring in spacetime. A theory may assign definite causal relationships to these localized interactions while retaining the quantum coherence responsible for the operational statistics.
Vilasini and Renner (2024) established an important result: under their assumptions, implementations of indefinite-order processes in a fixed classical spacetime that respect relativistic causality admit a finer-grained description with definite, acyclic causal order.
This demonstrates that operational indefinite order can be compatible with a finer-grained, definite causal structure under those assumptions. It does not, by itself, establish a single objectively realized event history.
The distinction matters because an effective operation is not necessarily identical to one uniquely localized spacetime event. It may represent a transformation implemented through multiple physical interactions or delocalized quantum degrees of freedom.
Consequently, an underlying description with definite spacetime causal relationships need not imply that the effective operations admit a classical mixture of definite orders.
This is not the same as saying that each run behaves like an A-before-B or a B-before-A circuit, with ordinary classical probabilities. That simpler explanation fails to reproduce quantum-switch interference, even in theories where some underlying variable, such as a particle’s path, does take one route.
3. Examples from single-outcome quantum theories
Two existing approaches illustrate how single-outcome ontologies can accommodate quantum interference without necessarily assigning a unique classical ordering to effective operations.
Bohmian mechanics
In Bohmian mechanics, particles have definite trajectories, while the guiding wave evolves through coherent alternatives.
Consider a quantum switch implemented using a massive particle whose spatial path controls whether it encounters operation A or B first. Before the paths recombine, the particle follows one actual route, but the interference observed at the output depends on the guiding wave associated with both routes.
Bohmian mechanics reproduces the standard quantum predictions for output-position measurements under quantum equilibrium.
Thus, a definite underlying trajectory does not make the experiment equivalent to an ordinary classical mixture of definite-order circuits.
GRW-type collapse theories
GRW-type collapse theories provide another approach. In the flash ontology, the realized history consists of definite spacetime events called flashes.
For a short experiment involving a microscopic particle, spontaneous collapses are extraordinarily unlikely, so quantum-switch interference can survive essentially unchanged.
If no collapse occurs during the coherent evolution, the collapse dynamics does not select either effective operational-order branch as uniquely realized. In the flash ontology, the history need not contain an event identifying one of those branches as the actual one.
This does not mean that the history establishes that neither operation came first, or that the underlying localized interactions lack definite causal relationships. It means that a definite history of flashes need not determine a unique classical ordering of the effective operations.
These examples distinguish definite realized events from definite classical orderings of effective operations. They illustrate possible single-outcome accounts, while a fully satisfactory relativistic implementation remains a further theoretical requirement.
4. What a single-outcome theory must demonstrate
Merely postulating one realized history is insufficient. A satisfactory theory must provide a consistent physical account of:
How the underlying events and interactions are realized.
How quantum coherence between alternative operational orders is preserved.
How the observed interference and causal-nonseparability statistics arise.
