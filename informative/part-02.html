(Andersson's, Galloway's and Howard's) "Cosmological Time (represented by the symbol τ)" is "the longest proper time along any causal path" or "the supremum of proper time along past-directed causal curves", which in a big-bang universe is the maximal proper time elapsed since the initial singularity. Regarding block-universe's "slice and loaf", a τ-slice is an instant of the universe as a whole in cosmological time. Our universe's cosmological time is gapless and each τ-slice has every part of our universe co-occurring during cosmological time. τ uses only the spacetime geometry. An alternative slice's "now" comes from synchronizing clocks with light signals in its own momentary rest frame, and it changes with its speed. Both slicings can be drawn, but they aren't on a par: one depends on who's doing the slicing, and the other doesn't. That's why τ works as a universal time standard. It isn't a clock that sets anyone's local rate, since local clocks keep their own proper time, but it's the common time every one of them sits under. One can call a τ-slice "one shared tick of the cosmic clock".
 
Reality, Information, and Knowledge
 
1. Reality and information about reality are different categories
 
A record of something is not the thing itself. A photograph of yesterday is not yesterday, a memory of an event is not the event, and a spacetime diagram containing an event is not the event occurring. CTGP builds this distinction into its formalism: its generated domain M(σ) represents the present world’s history mathematically, without implying that earlier stages continue to exist alongside the present. The signal-based epistemology makes the parallel point about knowledge: past events are inferred from present evidence rather than directly observed.
 
A description can be structurally faithful without being ontologically identical to what it describes. A map can accurately describe a city without containing the city, and a mathematical model can represent a physical system without constituting another instance of that system. Three things must therefore be distinguished:
 
referent ≠ representation ≠ instantiation
 
They can correspond without being identical.
 
2. Information is relational
 
Consider a rock that is photographed. There is the rock; the electromagnetic interaction producing the image; the physical image; the information it encodes; the observer’s interpretation; and the resulting proposition. Calling all six “the information” obscures important distinctions.
 
Information can be understood as involving relations between physical states and the distinctions they encode or make available. The same informational content can have different physical encodings: “HELLO” can be represented through ink, voltages, sound waves, magnetic domains, pixels, or neural activity.
 
Destroying one representation need not eliminate that information if other encodings remain. Destroying every accessible encoding may make the information permanently unrecoverable, although the corresponding fact need not cease to be true. Truth, representation, and accessibility are separate matters.
 
3. Knowledge depends on mediated access
 
The signal-based epistemology replaces “thing → knowledge” with a mediated chain:
 
thing → signal → detection → interpretation → knowledge
 
An observer does not simply possess reality. It processes physically available information and develops an understanding through interpretation and inference. Ignorance is not necessarily an intellectual failure; physical reality can impose limits on available information.
 
The framework identifies logical, physical, computational, and interpretive limits on knowledge. It defines knowledge as requiring sufficient confirmation while acknowledging that some truths cannot be verified.
 
Two principles follow:
 
\* A fact can be true without any observer being able to establish it.
\* A confident belief can exist without its content being true.
 
Confirmation is not the same as correctly interpreting what was confirmed
This is a subtle but powerful principle.
Suppose a child sees the Moon moving across the sky and concludes that the Moon is flying around Earth like a bird.
The child really did observe the Moon appearing at different locations over time. But the conclusion that the Moon is flying like a bird goes beyond what the observation itself establishes.
Or suppose a child notices that their shadow follows them wherever they go and concludes that the shadow likes to follow them.
Again, the observation is real. The interpretation is not thereby established.
This gives us a useful distinction:
Observing something, correctly identifying what was observed, and correctly explaining why it occurred are three different achievements that are not guaranteed to be achieved.
Even when an observation is accurate, the explanation can be mistaken.
And even when an explanation is plausible, it can remain unconfirmed.
This matters because people often describe themselves as having confirmed an entire explanation when they have actually confirmed only some of its components.
"For example:
'You observe a correlation, but that doesn’t establish causation.
You observe behavior, but that doesn’t establish the exact intention behind it.
You observe a physical representation, but that doesn’t establish that the representation is identical to what it represents.
You observe that a claim is widely accepted, but that doesn’t establish its truth.'"
The deeper lesson is that the evidential support for a conclusion cannot automatically exceed what the evidence and valid reasoning actually establish.
This doesn’t mean conclusions can never go beyond direct observation. Scientific reasoning routinely establishes things indirectly. It means that every additional inference needs appropriate justification.
Perhaps one of the most consequential intellectual achievements isn’t discovering a new fact, but learning to recognize exactly which fact has been established, which conclusion follows from it, and which additional conclusion has not yet been earned.
That skill doesn’t guarantee that we’ll discover the truth. It does help us avoid mistaking our interpretations, representations, and assumptions for the truth itself.
The world does not become more or less true according to how confidently we understand it. Our responsibility is to make our understanding answer to the world—not to make the world answer to our understanding.
 
4. Epistemic limits do not imply ontological indeterminacy
 
Suppose an event occurs and leaves no surviving detectable trace. “We cannot establish whether X happened” and “There is no fact about whether X happened” are different claims.
 
The signal-based framework keeps these apart. It allows that some truths may remain permanently inaccessible. The boundary of knowledge need not be the boundary of reality.
 
This works both ways: inability to detect X does not establish that X does not exist, while the possibility that X exists does not justify believing that it does.
 
Three epistemic conditions are worth distinguishing:
 
\* Known: sufficient confirming evidence is available.
\* Currently unknown: evidence might become available.
\* Structurally unknowable: the information required for confirmation cannot become available to the relevant observer or system.
 
Ignorance can therefore be temporary, permanent, or imposed by fundamental constraints.
 
5. One reality, many information channels
 
Observers differ in location, senses, history, computational capacity, causal access, and interpretive models. Consequently, they may possess different information about the same reality.
 
This does not require separate realities for separate observers. Differences in perception and belief can arise from differences in evidence, processing, and interpretation.
 
The position is neither naive realism nor the claim that everything is subjective: reality can be observer-independent while access to it remains perspectival and limited.
 
6. Coherence versus contact
 
A coherent model is internally consistent; an empirically successful model also makes contact with reality through observation, prediction, and testing.
 
Coherence alone does not establish truth. A logically consistent explanation can be wrong if its claims fail to correspond to the world.
 
The signal-based framework also describes useful explanations as portable “loop-packages”: representations that other minds can apply, test, refine, and use to support prediction or intervention.
 
Knowledge, on this approach, is not possession of a complete picture of reality. It is a sufficiently confirmed understanding developed through reliable interactions with available evidence.
 
Science accordingly involves constructing, testing, and improving models rather than merely accumulating descriptions.
 
7. Reality exceeds our models
 
If reality were identical to an observer’s current model, discovery and error would lose their ordinary distinction. Reality can surprise us precisely because our representations can be incomplete or mistaken.
 
Consider the progression:
 
causal access → information → representation → reconstruction → identity
 
None of these transitions is automatic.
 
Detecting a star does not mean describing it completely. Describing something does not mean physically reproducing it. Reproducing information about a person does not, by itself, establish that the person has been reproduced as the same individual.
 
The distinction between representation and instantiation is essential.
 
PART II. Causation, Time, and Becoming
 
8. The present as the place where reality is generated
 
Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism (CTGP) proposes that only the currently generated state of the universe exists. Earlier states do not persist as independently real regions of spacetime. What ceases to exist is each earlier state itself; the systems that were in those states may persist, subject to their own conditions of persistence.
 
The present physical state instead bears causal consequences of earlier events in its matter, fields, radiation, and other structures, some of which function as records. Present systems exhibit lawful physical continuity and may undergo transformations without preserving earlier states themselves.
 
The present is not merely whichever coordinate is labeled “now.” Under CTGP, it has ontological significance as the currently generated reality.
 
A memory of yesterday exists presently; yesterday does not thereby exist again. Radiation detected from a distant event exists presently; the earlier configuration responsible for its emission does not.
 
The past and future are asymmetric. Earlier events really occurred but no longer exist as events occurring. Future events have not yet been generated. The future is therefore not merely unknown; under CTGP, it does not yet exist.
 
9. What makes truths about the past true
 
Presentism faces a truthmaker problem: if earlier states no longer exist, what makes statements about them true?
 
CTGP adopts a Lucretian approach, associated with Bigelow (1996). The presently existing world instantiates primitive past-tensed properties expressing that particular things occurred at earlier stages.
 
These properties are attributed to the present world and, once acquired, are retained at subsequent stages.
 
Physical records serve a different function. They provide evidence of earlier events and can be damaged, altered, or erased. They are not themselves the fundamental truthmakers.
 
Erasing a record does not change what happened. It changes what observers can establish about what happened.
