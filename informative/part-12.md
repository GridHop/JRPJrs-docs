where Φ is the gravitational potential, negative inside a well. At present our motion relative to the cosmic background, about 370 km/s, costs our clocks roughly v²/2c² ≈ 7.6 × 10⁻⁷, about 24 seconds a year, and the gravitational wells we sit in add more. More generally, if Sₐ is the surface where τ = a, then for any event q with τ(q) ≥ a,

τ(q) = a + d(Sₐ, q)

where d(Sₐ, q) is the longest proper time along any causal curve from Sₐ to q. So between cosmic times a and b, no clock anywhere can accumulate more than b − a. Measured against cosmological time, local clocks run at or below it and never above. When you compare two local clocks with each other, one can look stretched relative to the other, but against the common time they all sit at or under the same ceiling.

Here's a way to picture it. In a universe with a Big Bang, imagine indestructible clocks started there and carried along every possible path, so there's one at every location and none ever stops. When you look at them, they disagree, because they've moved differently and sat in different gravitational wells. Now pick any event, look at all the clocks that could have gotten there, and take the one showing the most elapsed time. That reading is τ at that event. And that top reading is actually achieved, not just approached: when cosmological time is regular, a theorem guarantees a freely falling path from the initial singularity to every event that realizes the maximum. The clocks that come out on top are the ones that never fought the expansion and just rode along with it: the comoving ones. What makes their time common is that, in a homogeneous universe, all the comoving clocks read the same at the same cosmic moment everywhere, without anyone ever synchronizing them. They agree because they all started at the same beginning and the universe has been the same everywhere on average ever since. Every other clock reads less than that common value. So the "oldest clock" reading isn't any one clock's private time. It's the ceiling all of them share, and every local clock's shortfall is measured against it.

This isn't just a thought experiment, because the universe gives us something very close to that common clock: the cosmic microwave background. It's the light left over from when the universe became transparent, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, and it fills every location. It lines up with cosmological time in three ways.

First, it's the same in every direction to about one part in 100,000 once you take out the effect of our own motion, and its spectrum is the most perfect blackbody ever measured in nature. COBE's FIRAS instrument found deviations below about 50 parts per million of the peak, with a temperature today of T₀ = 2.72548 ± 0.00057 K. A blackbody that clean means the radiation was once in thermal equilibrium throughout the region we can see, which is the common hot early phase that the comoving clocks share. Uniformity that good is also what makes the idealized FLRW picture a good approximation to our universe, and that picture is where cosmological time coincides with the proper time of comoving clocks.

Second, the temperature works as a clock. As the universe expands, the radiation cools as T ∝ 1/a(t), so at redshift z it should be

T(z) = T₀ (1 + z)

That has been checked independently. Molecules in a galaxy at z = 0.89, excited by the background radiation, give 5.08 ± 0.10 K against a predicted 5.15 K. The Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect in 158 galaxy clusters out to z ≈ 1.35 constrains any departure of the form T(z) = T₀(1 + z)^(1−α) to α = 0.011 ± 0.016, consistent with zero. So, as far as the universe is uniform on large scales, which we can check only within our observable region, comoving observers who measure the same background temperature are at the same cosmic time, counted from the hot early phase, with no synchronization convention involved. Fitting the full CMB data within the standard model gives the cosmological time at our location, the age of the universe, as 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years. The oldest stars and globular clusters come out at roughly 12 to 13 billion years, younger than that ceiling, which is what you'd expect if no clock can have run longer than cosmological time.

Third, the CMB picks out the frame. On top of the uniform glow there's a dipole of about 3.36 mK, hotter in one direction and colder in the opposite one. That's the Doppler signature of our own motion at about 370 km/s relative to the comoving frame. Planck even detected the aberration and Doppler boosting of the small-scale pattern that this motion should produce, which confirms that the dipole is mostly our motion and not something built into the radiation. The frame in which the dipole vanishes is the frame of the comoving clocks, whose proper time is cosmological time.

"Perfectly" would be too strong, though, and the reasons are worth knowing. The one-part-in-100,000 ripples are real. They're the seeds of galaxies, and they're why the match between cosmological time and comoving clocks holds only approximately in our actual, lumpy universe. There's also an open puzzle. Counts of distant quasars and radio galaxies show a dipole pointing roughly the same way as the CMB's, though how closely depends on the sample, but about two to two and a half times larger than our motion should produce, at around five sigma. A 2026 analysis of about 637,000 active galaxies still finds it. Its authors suspect local structure and selection effects, and consider a genuine mismatch between the rest frames of matter and radiation unlikely. Even if such a mismatch held up, it would complicate the picture without removing cosmological time. τ is defined by the spacetime geometry, and the CMB is the strongest evidence we have that the geometry is close to the uniform kind in which τ is the comoving clocks' time. None of this proves the τ-slices are "the present." What it does show is that the common frame, and a common time counted from the hot Big Bang epoch, are physical features of our universe, not conventions anyone chose. That this common time is τ itself, counted from a beginning, holds on the standard model.

This is where Einstein's relativity of simultaneity comes in, and I want to be careful, because the relativity of simultaneity isn't wrong about what it actually says. In 1905 Einstein pointed out that we have no common time for two distant places A and B until we define one, and he defined it by stipulating that light takes as long to go from A to B as from B to A. Synchronize clocks that way in one inertial frame, do the same in a frame moving at speed v relative to the first, and the two sets of clocks disagree about which distant events happen at the same time. The Lorentz transformation shows it directly:

t′ = γ (t − v x / c²),  γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)

so two events with Δt = 0 but separated by Δx get Δt′ = −γ v Δx / c², which isn't zero. That's correct, and no experiment done inside a sealed lab will tell you which frame's synchronization is the right one, because the laws are the same in all of them.

The misconception is taking that synchronization procedure, which is local clock time extended across space by a convention, as if it were what "at the same time" means. Take that as the definition, and the relativity of simultaneity turns into the conclusion that there's no physical fact at all about which distant events are happening at the same time. Einstein himself leaned that way late in life, writing that the distinction between past, present and future is "only a stubbornly persistent illusion." But that conclusion follows only if light-signal synchronization within some inertial frame is the only thing "at the same time" could mean. The spacetime of special relativity is empty and perfectly symmetric, so naturally nothing in it singles out one frame; it doesn't even have a finite cosmological time. Our universe isn't empty. It has matter and radiation with a rest frame of their own, and we measure our velocity relative to that frame from the dipole in the microwave background. On the standard model, its geometry also has a regular cosmological time, and that gives a sense of "at the same time" that uses no signaling convention at all: the comoving clocks are in step because of their common beginning and the universe's large-scale uniformity, not because anyone exchanged light pulses. The τ function and its slices are facts about the geometry. What's proposed is reading "same τ" as "at the same time." On that proposal, two events are co-present when they have the same τ, and that doesn't depend on any observer's choice of frame. The laws stay exactly as Lorentz invariant as before. What picks out the cosmic frame is the actual contents and shape of the universe, not the laws, which is why the sealed-lab argument never touches it. To be fair about the limits, relativity guarantees that a spacetime like ours can be sliced into whole-universe moments, but in many ways, and it doesn't pick one. Cosmological time picks one from the geometry alone, unique up to relabeling and independent of any coordinates or observers, which makes it geometrically distinguished. Treating it as the real "now" goes a step further: relativity permits that but doesn't force it, and τ isn't the only convention-free candidate. In many cosmological spacetimes the geometry also fixes a slicing by surfaces of constant mean curvature. That slicing agrees with τ in idealized FLRW but generally differs in a lumpy universe. What singles τ out is that it's the ceiling on every clock's elapsed proper time, so preferring it is part of the philosophical commitment. What relativity gives with no commitment is that wherever τ is regular, it exists, is fixed by the geometry, and bounds every clock's elapsed time.
