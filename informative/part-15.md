What makes this work without any signals is that τ(p) is fixed entirely by p's own causal past, since it's a supremum over curves running back from p. τ is one function on the whole spacetime, each event gets its value from its own past, and the slices it defines span regions that have never been in contact. Regularity guarantees that τ runs down to the same limiting value, zero, along every worldline traced back, even though they needn't run back to one common point. And in a universe that's close to uniform on large scales, as the CMB shows ours is, equal τ also marks the same epoch of the large-scale expansion, with the same background temperature to about one part in 100,000, in regions that never exchanged a signal, even though local conditions, a galaxy versus a void, can differ enormously. The simultaneity τ defines doesn't depend on any observer or choice: which events share a τ-slice is fixed by the geometry.

The slice is also complete in the causal sense. Every causal path traced back from a later event runs through it, so with well-posed laws, a slice regular enough for the equations in question, and suitable data on it (in general relativity, data that satisfy its constraint equations), those data determine everything later in the loaf, or its probabilities where chance is involved. The data include instantaneous rates (velocities, momenta, how fast fields are changing), so what's under way in each part is part of the slice too. "Something occurring" in every part is meant literally, not just a frozen arrangement.

Co-occurring at the same cosmic time doesn't mean having the same local age, though. Along any worldline, local proper time runs at or below cosmic time. In FLRW, for a clock with peculiar velocity v relative to the local comoving observers,

dτ_local = dt √(1 − v²/c²)

and more generally, if Sₐ is the slice τ = a, then for any event q with τ(q) ≥ a,

τ(q) = a + d(Sₐ, q)

where d(Sₐ, q) is the longest proper time along any causal curve from Sₐ to q. So between slices a and b, nothing accumulates more than b − a. An astronaut back from a fast trip and her stay-at-home twin are on the same slice, co-occurring, with different ages. Our own motion through the cosmic background costs our clocks roughly v²/2c² ≈ 7.6 × 10⁻⁷, about 24 seconds a year relative to comoving clocks on the same slices, before counting gravity and our other motions.

In the block, every τ-slice exists equally. The loaf is the universe as a whole, and a τ-slice is one full-width instant of it, so co-occurring just means sharing a slice. None of that needs a special now, and the standard block doesn't have one.

It's worth being clear about what the geometry settles. That the τ-slices exist, that their values run unbroken, and that they hold exactly one moment of every part are facts about the loaf, and so is the simultaneity they define. What the geometry doesn't settle is whether τ-slices are privileged over other ways of cutting the loaf. τ isn't the only geometry-defined candidate. In many cosmological spacetimes there's also a slicing by surfaces of constant mean curvature, which agrees with τ in an idealized uniform universe but not in a lumpy one. What singles τ out is that it's the ceiling on every clock's elapsed proper time. But the question here doesn't need τ to be privileged, only that it's there.

Finally, whether our universe has a regular τ at all is open. The standard cosmological model gives one, but only by extrapolating back past anything observations test. Some proposals for the earliest epochs, such as a bounce after an endless contraction, could make τ infinite. And part of that openness is permanent: regularity is a property of the whole spacetime, including regions we can never observe, so observations can only ever support it through a model. Even so, the standard model assigns only minutes to everything before the earliest epoch we test directly, the formation of the first light nuclei. Counting from that nearly uniform epoch instead, the model describes everything since with the same continuous cosmic time, so the picture of an unbroken succession of slices holds over the span the model covers, whatever came before, though that's still a model-based conclusion rather than a proof about the whole spacetime.

So, to the question. On the standard model, our universe's cosmological time is gapless in the sense that matters at the scales general relativity describes: its values fill an unbroken interval with no first instant, and every worldline passes through every slice between any two it reaches, exactly once. Whether time stays continuous at the Planck scale is open, although even in a discrete world with whole-universe instants, "each instant follows the one before with nothing in between" could still hold. And each τ-slice is an instant of the universe as a whole in which every part that exists at that cosmic time has something occurring: one moment each, all at the same τ, none able to affect another, with different local ages, together forming a state of the universe that everything later traces back through. None of that depends on a choice of coordinates. It's carried by the metric itself, which is as much a part of our universe's content as its matter.
