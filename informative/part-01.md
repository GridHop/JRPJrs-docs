# Informative

A conscious existence’s physical substrate continuously constrains what experiences are physically possible for that conscious existence. The substrate is not merely a passive container for experience: its physical organization, properties, and ongoing activity place constraints on the range of experiences that can occur.

In humans, relevant constraints include the organization and functioning of sensory systems, neural architecture, and physiological condition. These help constrain which sensory experiences, thoughts, emotions, and other conscious states are possible. Such constraints can change—for example, through development, injury, sleep, medication, or other changes in the body and brain.

The substrate does not, by itself, specify every detail of an individual's actual experience. Environmental conditions, sensory inputs, memories, and ongoing mental processes also matter. The substrate constrains what can occur; the interaction of the substrate with these other factors helps determine what does occur.

An individual's own mental interpretations can also causally contribute to that individual's emotions. For example, two people might interpret the same ambiguous situation differently: one interprets it as threatening, while the other interprets it as harmless. Those interpretations can contribute to different emotional responses.

This causal relationship is not strictly one-way. Emotions can also influence attention, interpretation, memory, and reasoning. Interpretations are not necessarily consciously chosen, nor are they the sole causes of emotions; physiology, automatic processing, prior learning, and circumstances can contribute as well.

Thus, the physical substrate continuously constrains the range of experiences that are physically possible, while ongoing physical, cognitive, and psychological processes—including an individual's interpretations—help determine the experiences that actually occur. Interpretations are causally relevant parts of the processes producing emotions, not merely passive descriptions of them.
