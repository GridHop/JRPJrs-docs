That also means time dilation remains fully intact. Two observers can be globally co-present at one cosmological-time level and again at another while accumulating different amounts of proper time along their respective worldlines.
The relevant proper time along a timelike worldline is determined by the spacetime metric and the path taken, not merely by the fact that two events belong to the same global slice.
Likewise, global simultaneity doesn’t imply instantaneous communication. Two events can belong to the same spacelike hypersurface without being able to exchange a physical signal at that stage. Co-presence and causal interaction are different relationships.
There are important limitations to CTGP.
Its proposed identification of cosmological-time slices with the ontological present is an interpretive commitment, not a theorem of general relativity. The actual universe has not been established to satisfy every global condition required by the framework, and the cosmological-time construction isn’t guaranteed to provide the necessary foliation in arbitrary spacetimes.
CTGP is also not an experimentally confirmed theory of temporal becoming or a completed relativistic quantum theory. Its intended compatibility with established relativistic predictions doesn’t independently establish its ontology.
The framework does make commitments that could be challenged by future physics. In particular, it requires an adequate forward-generative account in which the present physical state and governing laws suffice to determine or probabilistically generate its successor without indispensable independent future boundary information.
Confirmed physically realized closed timelike curves would conflict with its assumed global causal structure. Genuine interventionist retrocausality or physically indispensable future boundary conditions that cannot be accommodated by an adequate forward-generative account would conflict with its generability requirement.
Those possibilities haven’t been experimentally established as features of the universe, and their absence doesn’t prove CTGP correct.
It’s also worth distinguishing CTGP from some other proposed interpretations of time. Ordinary presentism says only the present exists but doesn’t, by itself, explain how a global present should be identified in relativistic spacetime. Growing-block theories retain earlier events as existing, whereas CTGP holds that earlier states genuinely existed but have ceased to exist as those states. Moving-spotlight theories assign a distinguished present within an ontology that generally retains all times, so they aren’t alternatives to the block universe in the same ontological sense.
These differences matter because a theory can accommodate relativity’s mathematics while interpreting the existence of the represented events differently.
So I think the answer depends on what the questioner wants an alternative to accomplish.
If the question is simply whether simultaneity can be described without accepting the block universe, the answer is yes. Special and general relativity already provide mathematical descriptions of simultaneity without requiring an additional assertion that every represented time physically exists.
If the question is whether an alternative can preserve a meaningful concept of objective temporal becoming, then local becoming, growing-block approaches, causal-set growth, and various forms of presentism offer different possibilities, although they don’t all provide a global simultaneity relation.
And if the question specifically asks whether there can be an objectively distinguished global present without abandoning relativistic physics, cosmological time supplies a mathematically serious candidate under suitable conditions, while CTGP proposes one ontological interpretation of that structure.
The distinction I’d emphasize is that relativity determines how simultaneity, proper time, and causal relationships are mathematically and experimentally described. It does not, by those results alone, establish whether past, present, and future all physically coexist.
The block universe is one interpretation of relativistic spacetime. CTGP is a proposed alternative that treats the global present as the currently generated cosmological-time state, with earlier states having genuinely existed but no longer existing as those states.
Whether that interpretation is ultimately correct remains open. But accounting for simultaneity does not, by itself, require adopting eternalism.

Via math alone, can mathematical theories have the math that reveal each event's every specific of the past, present, and future, or are such theories' math only able to reveal "constraints on possible existence" but with Laplace's-demon limitations?

Via math alone, meaning a theory's equations and laws without any empirical input, no, and that holds for past, present, and future events alike. A physical theory's math narrows down which events could possibly be the case at any time. But none of our current established theories determines every specific event by its laws alone, since each needs initial conditions and parameters that its laws don't supply. Something other than the laws has to supply those. In some theories, even that doesn't narrow things to a single possibility per time, whether that time is past, present, or future.

The simplest way to see this is Newton's second law, F = ma. Take a free particle, so F = 0. Then a = 0, and integrating twice gives x(t) = x₀ + v₀t. The equation has done real work: it rules out the particle speeding up, slowing down, or curving. But x₀ and v₀ are left completely open. Every choice of them gives a different trajectory, and every one of those trajectories satisfies the law equally well. So at any given t, whether it's an earlier time, the present, or a later one, the law alone leaves infinitely many candidate positions. Notice that this includes the present: the law doesn't tell you where the particle is now any more than where it was or will be.

A spring works the same way. From m d²x/dt² = −kx you get x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), with ω = √(k/m). The math fixes the frequency, which is a genuine constraint. The amplitude A and phase φ, though, are only pinned down once you specify the spring's position and velocity at some time, and nothing in the equation itself supplies those.

Scale that up and nothing changes in principle. Einstein's field equations, G_μν = (8πG/c⁴)T_μν, have an enormous space of solutions: black holes, expanding universes, gravitational waves, and plenty of exotic spacetimes nobody thinks are physically realistic. General relativity actually makes the narrowing unusually explicit. The data you specify on a slice of spacetime, including the slice you'd call the present, has to satisfy the Hamiltonian and momentum constraint equations. Not every configuration is admissible, but infinitely many still are.

On top of that, dimensionless constants like the fine-structure constant (about 1/137 at low energies) are measured, not derived. The Standard Model alone has 19 such free parameters in its basic form, and around 26 once neutrino masses are included.

Now set up a complete initial-value problem: the equations plus fully specified data at one time, say the present. A deterministic theory with well-behaved equations then returns exactly one solution, and it fixes things in both directions. Plug measured values of x₀ and v₀ into x(t) = x₀ + v₀t and you get one position for every t, earlier as well as later. That's the Laplace's demon idea: an intellect for whom both past and future would be calculable from the present state.

It's perfectly good mathematics, but notice what it leaves out. The present state is the input. The demon doesn't get the specifics of the present from the math at all. It gets them from the data, and only then does the math fix the specifics at other times.

Even with complete data, uniqueness isn't guaranteed. Norton's dome is the standard example. A ball rests at the apex of a dome shaped so that, in suitable units, its equation of motion is d²r/dt² = √r. One solution has the ball sit there forever, r(t) = 0. But for any later time T you like, there is also a solution where the ball sits at r = 0 until T and then follows r(t) = (t − T)⁴/144. You can check it. For t ≥ T the second derivative is (t − T)²/12, which is exactly √r, and position and velocity both match up smoothly at T.

The equation is also unchanged when you run time backward, so the mirror-image solutions work too. A ball resting at the apex now is equally consistent with having sat there forever, or with having rolled up and come to rest there at any earlier time T, following r(t) = (T − t)⁴/144 until it arrives. Same law, same present data, infinitely many possible futures and infinitely many possible pasts.

The dome is a special construction, and people still argue about how much it tells us about realistic Newtonian systems. Mathematically, though, it's a clean counterexample to guaranteed uniqueness. General relativity has an analogous issue in certain spacetimes with Cauchy horizons, beyond which the data on a slice no longer fixes a unique continuation. That's a feature of particular solutions, not of the theory across the board.

Determinism also isn't the same as predictability. In chaotic systems a small error δ₀ in your data typically grows roughly like δ₀e^(λt) over a range of times, where λ is the Lyapunov exponent. So "fixed in principle" doesn't mean "knowable in practice." Running the equations backward is no better: in typical chaotic systems, errors grow just as quickly when you retrodict as when you predict.

Quantum mechanics adds another layer. The Schrödinger equation, iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ, is deterministic for ψ in both directions: given ψ at one time, it's fixed at every earlier and later time. But what ψ tells you about actual measurement outcomes is probabilities, via the Born rule P(a) = |⟨a|ψ⟩|². So in its standard formulation, even complete present data narrows a future measurement's outcome only to a weighted set of candidates. Retrodiction has a parallel problem. On readings where measurement collapses ψ, many different earlier states could have led to the same post-measurement state, so present data doesn't single out earlier specifics either.

Whether the underlying physics is genuinely indeterministic depends on interpretation. Bohmian mechanics adds definite particle positions plus a guidance equation that steers them. That gets you single outcomes, but only once you feed in exact particle positions as further data. Many-worlds keeps only the Schrödinger equation and says every outcome occurs on some branch, though how probability fits into that picture is itself debated. Collapse theories such as GRW go the other way, building genuine randomness into the dynamics itself. The math alone doesn't decide between these.

Could a theory ever fix every specific, past, present, and future, by its laws alone? Only if its equations admitted exactly one solution, with no free data and no free constants. Even then, for a quantum theory a unique state fixes probabilities for outcomes, so whether that counts as fixing every specific event depends on interpretation.
