The shift in simultaneity is Δt′ = γ v Δx / c², and Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away, so for motion along that line it comes to about γ(v/c) × 2.5 million years. “Thousands of years” corresponds to a few hundred km/s, roughly Earth’s own speed relative to the cosmic background; 370 km/s gives about 3,000 years. A neutrino moving near light speed would put those events millions of years apart or more.

None of this makes the τ-slice an absolute “now” in the block universe, where every slice exists equally and none is special. But “not an absolute now” and “just a convenient coordinate choice” are different claims, and only the first is true.

A clock can still be deliberately set to τ: read the local CMB temperature to find your cosmic time, and use the dipole to correct for your motion.

There's a real-world precedent. GPS satellite clocks are deliberately rate-adjusted at the factory, because otherwise they'd drift about 38 microseconds a day from ground time. A clock built to track τ would work the same way: it would keep its own proper time but correct its rate for its motion relative to the CMB and for local gravity.

Devices don't keep a server's time on their own; they sync to it by design, and local clocks relate to τ the same way.

One practical aside: the CMB temperature is currently measured to about 2 parts in 10,000, which pins down cosmic time only to within roughly 3 million years. A practical τ-clock would therefore set itself once from cosmology and then keep time through rate corrections, much like a device that syncs once and then ticks on its own. As a wall clock, the CMB is a little slow to update.

Such is a way to construct a terrestrial device that uses observations of the CMB to estimate the universe’s cosmological epoch, correct for motion relative to the cosmological rest frame, and maintain a time display through local clock-rate corrections.
