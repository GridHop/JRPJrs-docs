It's worth being clear about what the geometry settles. That the τ-slices exist, that their values run unbroken, and that they hold exactly one moment of every part are facts about the loaf, and so is the simultaneity they define. What the geometry doesn't settle is whether τ-slices are privileged over other ways of cutting the loaf. τ isn't the only geometry-defined candidate. In many cosmological spacetimes there's also a slicing by surfaces of constant mean curvature, which agrees with τ in an idealized uniform universe but not in a lumpy one. What singles τ out is that it's the ceiling on every clock's elapsed proper time. But the question here doesn't need τ to be privileged, only that it's there.

Finally, whether our universe has a regular τ at all is open. The standard cosmological model gives one, but only by extrapolating back past anything observations test. Some proposals for the earliest epochs, such as a bounce after an endless contraction, could make τ infinite. And part of that openness is permanent: regularity is a property of the whole spacetime, including regions we can never observe, so observations can only ever support it through a model. Even so, the standard model assigns only minutes to everything before the earliest epoch we test directly, the formation of the first light nuclei. Counting from that nearly uniform epoch instead, the model describes everything since with the same continuous cosmic time, so the picture of an unbroken succession of slices holds over the span the model covers, whatever came before, though that's still a model-based conclusion rather than a proof about the whole spacetime.

So, to the question. On the standard model, our universe's cosmological time is gapless in the sense that matters at the scales general relativity describes: its values fill an unbroken interval with no first instant, and every worldline passes through every slice between any two it reaches, exactly once. Whether time stays continuous at the Planck scale is open, although even in a discrete world with whole-universe instants, "each instant follows the one before with nothing in between" could still hold. And each τ-slice is an instant of the universe as a whole in which every part that exists at that cosmic time has something occurring: one moment each, all at the same τ, none able to affect another, with different local ages, together forming a state of the universe that everything later traces back through. None of that depends on a choice of coordinates. It's carried by the metric itself, which is as much a part of our universe's content as its matter.

Cosmological time isn’t a coordinate choice. Andersson, Galloway and Howard (1998) define it at each event p as

τ(p) = sup { L(λ) : λ a past-directed causal curve from p }

the longest proper time along any causal path reaching p from its past. That uses only the spacetime geometry: no coordinates, no observers, no velocities. Every observer, comoving or not, gets the same τ at the same event. What is a coordinate choice is the neutrino’s slice. Its “now” comes from synchronizing clocks with light signals in its own momentary rest frame, and it changes with its speed. Both slicings can be drawn, but they aren’t on a par: one depends on who’s doing the slicing, and the other doesn’t. The neutrino’s slice is still a valid way of labeling events, but what exists of it is only where it meets the current τ-slice. That’s why τ works as a universal time standard. It isn’t a clock that sets anyone’s local rate, since local clocks keep their own proper time, but it’s the common time every one of them sits under. One can call a τ-slice “one shared tick of the cosmic clock,” and that’s exactly right, as long as “tick” means one instant of a gapless succession rather than a discrete beat with gaps between beats.

The shift in simultaneity is Δt′ = γ v Δx / c², and Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away, so for motion along that line it comes to about γ(v/c) × 2.5 million years. “Thousands of years” corresponds to a few hundred km/s, roughly Earth’s own speed relative to the cosmic background; 370 km/s gives about 3,000 years. A neutrino moving near light speed would put those events millions of years apart or more.

None of this makes the τ-slice an absolute “now” in the block universe, where every slice exists equally and none is special. But “not an absolute now” and “just a convenient coordinate choice” are different claims, and only the first is true.

A clock can still be deliberately set to τ: read the local CMB temperature to find your cosmic time, and use the dipole to correct for your motion.

There's a real-world precedent. GPS satellite clocks are deliberately rate-adjusted at the factory, because otherwise they'd drift about 38 microseconds a day from ground time. A clock built to track τ would work the same way: it would keep its own proper time but correct its rate for its motion relative to the CMB and for local gravity.

Devices don't keep a server's time on their own; they sync to it by design, and local clocks relate to τ the same way.

One practical aside: the CMB temperature is currently measured to about 2 parts in 10,000, which pins down cosmic time only to within roughly 3 million years. A practical τ-clock would therefore set itself once from cosmology and then keep time through rate corrections, much like a device that syncs once and then ticks on its own. As a wall clock, the CMB is a little slow to update.

Such is a way to construct a terrestrial device that uses observations of the CMB to estimate the universe’s cosmological epoch, correct for motion relative to the cosmological rest frame, and maintain a time display through local clock-rate corrections.
