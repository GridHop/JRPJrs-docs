A drifting clock is a separate problem, and it needs less. A real clock, especially a quantum one, can misbehave. Picture a quantum clock whose pointer hops around a ring of positions, advancing on average but with some probability of hopping backward. Record its readings at two successive events and the later event can carry the earlier reading. If identity ran through clock readings, a bad clock could change what persisted, which is absurd. What the drifting clock gets wrong first is the causal order. Along one worldline, which of two events is later is fixed by the light-cone structure, in any spacetime with a consistent future direction and no closed timelike curves, whether or not τ is regular. And τ agrees with that order on every worldline at once, because it can't run backward along any of them. If q lies to the future of p along a timelike curve of length ℓ, then any path from the beginning to p, extended by that curve, is a path from the beginning to q that's longer by ℓ, so
 
τ(q) ≥ τ(p) + ℓ > τ(p)
 
It also increases along light-like paths. So τ goes up along every future-directed causal path, and the later event really has the larger τ, whatever the clock said. The commonality does its real work beyond a single worldline: it compares durations across different worldlines and says which distant events are co-present. τ isn't one more clock that might also be wrong. It's the common time every clock is measured against, and its authority comes from the geometry, not from any one device. The twins' clocks can disagree by years while the order of what happened to each twin is never in doubt, because that order is causal. One caveat: τ settles the fact, not our knowledge of it. We get at τ only through measurement (comoving clocks, the background temperature, cosmological models), and "13.8 billion years" has error bars. And in quantum settings where the clocks themselves are in superposition, how to recover a definite order of events from clock data alone is still an open problem. The fact is definite, but our access to it goes through the same kind of measurement it judges.
 
So, back to identity. Persisting means being the same thing across successive stages, and that can't even be stated without an order. Causal inheritance has a direction: "B came from A" and "A came from B" involve the same two states and differ only in order. Fission, one thing becoming two, and fusion, two things becoming one, can involve the very same states in opposite orders. "Uninterrupted" is a claim about the structure of the order, and a thing's end is its last stage, which needs an order to be last in. So the order is necessary.
 
But it isn't sufficient. A perfect duplicate sits in exactly the same order as the original. Or take a uranium-238 nucleus that decays at some random moment. The order is fixed by what actually happened: it decayed then and not before. But what persisted? The U-238 nucleus has come to its end, its nucleons carry on, and a thorium nucleus and an alpha particle begin. One order gives three different answers, so something other than the order has to be deciding them. That something is the persistence conditions of each kind of thing: what a thing of that kind has to satisfy to stay itself across successive stages. They differ by kind. An organism, a hurricane, a nucleus and a field all persist under different conditions, and something that can't end has no termination condition at all. This is also why a state isn't the same as the thing it's a state of. The state at a given time ceases even if a qualitatively identical state follows it, while the thing whose state it was carries on as long as its conditions are met, unless it has undergone its end. Chance doesn't change any of this. Even when the next state is random, it still arises from the state before it under laws that set the probabilities. The order is fixed by what occurred, but the order alone still doesn't tell you what continued.
 
For people, the persistence conditions are genuinely open. Psychological continuity alone can't be it. Suppose one person split into two successors with the same memories and personality, and continuity guaranteed identity. Then both successors would be the original. Since identity is transitive (A = B and A = C gives B = C), they'd also be the same person as each other, which they plainly aren't. That's Parfit's fission problem. Causal ancestry alone can't be it either, since a child is causally descended from a parent without being the parent. A plausible candidate is uninterrupted causal inheritance of an embodied organization, as opposed to reconstruction from a record of it. But that's a proposal, not a result, and the answer may be partly a matter of which criterion we choose rather than a fact waiting to be discovered.
 
Magnitude matters only where a kind's conditions mention duration. A hurricane or a person doesn't need to last a certain number of hours to stay itself; a 24-hour ceasefire does. And the relabeling point from earlier shows that identity doesn't depend on the numbers on the labels at all. Replace τ with any σ = F(τ) where F′ > 0 and every identity verdict stays the same. The order is untouched, and so are physical durations like proper time, which are the ones a duration-sensitive kind's conditions refer to.
 
As for the act of measuring, it's neither necessary nor sufficient. Things persist whether or not anyone measures anything. Measuring presupposes identity rather than producing it: a reading of 9:00 and a later reading of 10:00 measure an hour only if they came from the same clock. Swap the clock for an identical-looking replacement in between and you have two readings from two clocks, not an hour. Measurement can also get the order wrong, as the drifting clock shows. Measurement belongs on the evidence side. Records can establish which of two successors went through which procedure, but deciding which one, if either, is the original still takes a theory of persistence. And every measurement happens in the present: comparisons with earlier times go through present records, and comparisons with later times go through the present state plus the laws.
 
Finally, a block universe doesn't get out of any of this. Cosmological time is defined by the geometry of spacetime, and someone who holds that past, present and future all exist accepts that same spacetime. On the standard model, that means the same τ with the same values, the same ceiling on every clock, the same age of the universe, and the same rest frame in the microwave background. A drifting clock in a block universe gets the order wrong for exactly the same reason. The only difference is what the τ-slices are taken to be. In the block, every slice exists, so the present isn't the only existence of all duration. The order is simply there rather than something that happens, and no slice is singled out as now. Someone holding that view can refuse to treat any τ-slice as the present, but they can't refuse τ itself, any more than they can refuse the age of the universe. If they set it aside, "the state of the universe at a time" stays relative to however they choose to slice spacetime. The account of identity carries over unchanged: order plus persistence conditions. Having every stage exist doesn't settle which stages are stages of one thing. The persistence conditions still decide that.
 
So, to the question: yes. Numerical identity across times is via the measure of time plus the persistence conditions of the kind of thing in question. The measure of time here means time's order and, where a kind's conditions require it, time's magnitude, both fixed by cosmological time as one common, definite standard in any spacetime where it's regular, the standard model of ours included. Where it isn't, the order along each worldline is still fixed by the causal structure and durations along it by proper time, but a single common standard needs a physically distinguished slice to count from. The order is necessary but not sufficient, and the persistence conditions supply what the order can't, though they only apply across an order. The act of measuring time is neither necessary nor sufficient: it presupposes the identity of the clock doing the measuring, it can be wrong, and it answers to the actual order and durations, which cosmological time fixes. It's how we come to know identity, always from the present, not what makes it so. The same holds in a block universe, where cosmological time is just as available and every stage exists.
 
Regarding block-universe's "slice & loaf", a τ-slice is an instant of the universe as a whole in cosmological time. Is our universe's cosmological time gapless & does each τ-slice have every part of our universe co-occurring during cosmological time?
 
Regarding the standard cosmological model, yes to both, though each "yes" needs pinning down. Cosmological time is continuous, so its slices form an unbroken succession. No cosmic time is skipped, and no worldline jumps over any slice. Whether time stays continuous at the very smallest scales is a separate and open question. And each τ-slice contains exactly one moment of every part of the universe that exists at that cosmic time, all at the same value of τ, none able to affect another, together making a state of the universe that everything later traces back through. Here's the longer version.
 
In the block picture, spacetime is a loaf. All of it, past, present and future together, is the universe as a whole, and a slice is a three-dimensional cross-section: the whole of space at one instant. The catch relativity adds is that the loaf doesn't come pre-sliced. Observers moving relative to each other cut it at different angles, because their clocks, synchronized by light signals in their own frames, disagree about which distant events are simultaneous:
 
t′ = γ (t − v x / c²), γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)
 
so two events with Δt = 0, a distance Δx apart, get Δt′ = −γ v Δx / c². Nothing in the laws picks one cut over another. What can pick one is the geometry of the particular loaf, and that's where cosmological time comes in.
